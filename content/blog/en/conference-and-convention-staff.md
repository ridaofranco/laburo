A conference or convention isn't one big event: it's several small events happening at once, in different rooms, with the same audience moving between them every forty minutes. That's why the staff isn't put together like a party's. This guide covers the positions you need, how they're deployed by moment and by room, and the mistakes that keep repeating.

## The moments of the day, not the total attendance

The first step is to stop thinking "we're this many". A conference has moments with very different needs:

- **The morning opening.** Almost the whole audience arrives in the same window and has to register before the first talk. It's the biggest peak of the day.
- **The talk sessions.** The audience splits across rooms. Each room needs someone inside.
- **The room changes.** For a few minutes, everyone moves at once through corridors and stairways.
- **The coffee break and lunch.** People gather in one place and want to be served quickly.
- **The close.** Final plenary, cloakroom and departure.

Each moment needs a different number of people at each post. How to do the maths by real pace is in [how many staff do you need for an event](/en/blog/how-many-staff-for-an-event), and how to build shifts so nobody gets overstretched is in [shift planning for long events](/en/blog/shift-planning-for-long-events).

## The positions, one by one

### Registration

It's the first impression and the first bottleneck. Registration staff look the attendee up in the system, hand over a badge or wristband and sort out the cases that aren't on the list. It's worth separating the desk for pre-registered attendees from on-site registration and from special cases (speakers, press, guests). The detail of the role is in [what conference registration staff do](/en/blog/conference-registration-staff-role).

### Reception and wayfinding

Receptionists, hostesses and ushers welcome people, tell them which room to go to and answer whatever the programme doesn't make clear. During room changes they stand in corridors and at junctions, not behind a desk. The profile is described in [corporate event hostess and reception](/en/blog/corporate-event-hostess-and-reception).

### Room assistants

At least one per room, during each session. They control entry if the room has a capacity limit, take the microphone to the audience for questions, tell the speaker how much time is left, report any technical fault and get the room ready for the next session. It's the most underestimated position: without it, production ends up running from room to room.

### Plenary and stage

If there's a main room with a stage, someone needs to make sure each speaker goes on on time, with their presentation loaded and the microphone ready. Depending on the size, that's a stage manager or a show caller. What they do is in [what is a stage manager at an event](/en/blog/what-is-a-stage-manager-at-an-event).

### Technical crew

Sound, video and, if the conference is broadcast, streaming. At conferences with several rooms, each room needs at least someone who can handle the tech, or a technician covering several nearby rooms. The technical side often comes with the equipment supplier or the venue: ask in writing before counting people.

### Translation and interpreting

If there are speakers in another language, you add interpreters. Simultaneous interpreters work in pairs and the booth is a separate supplier. General staff take care of handing out and collecting the receivers. All the detail is in [conference interpreters and translators](/en/blog/conference-interpreters-and-translators).

### Runners

They take whatever is missing to wherever it's missing: a battery, a cable, a lost speaker, a box of badges. At a conference with many rooms they're what stops someone abandoning their post to go and fetch something. What the job involves is in [what does an event runner do](/en/blog/what-does-an-event-runner-do).

### Coffee break, lunch and cloakroom

Waiters, bar or restocking staff and dishwashers, if the caterer doesn't bring its own people. The cloakroom matters most in the morning and at the close. For that post, see [how to run an event cloakroom](/en/blog/how-to-run-an-event-cloakroom).

### Coordination

With so many posts spread across different rooms, someone has to keep an eye on the whole team. At conferences with several rooms it's worth having a lead per area and a coordinator above them. The signs that you need one and how to set up the chain are in [when you need an event staff coordinator](/en/blog/when-you-need-an-event-staff-coordinator).

## How the team moves during the day

People don't stay fixed at one post all day. A common pattern:

1. **Early on**, almost the whole guest service team is at registration and reception.
2. **When the sessions start**, part of that team moves to room assistant duties and wayfinding in the corridors.
3. **During the coffee break**, room assistants get the rooms ready and some reinforce wayfinding.
4. **At the close**, the extra hands go back to the cloakroom and the exit.

That movement has to be written into the schedule and explained in the briefing. Otherwise, by mid-morning you have five people at an empty desk and rooms with nobody in them.

## Common mistakes

- **Sizing by attendees rather than by moment.** The team that's enough for the rooms isn't enough for morning registration.
- **Rooms with no assistant.** The speaker ends up shouting for the microphone.
- **Not planning for room changes.** Corridors with no wayfinding and sessions that start late.
- **Counting on the venue's technical crew without confirming it in writing.**
- **A generic brief.** Each post needs to know its room, its hours, who it reports to and what it does at each moment of the day.

When someone asks how much the venue costs, the answer can't be a number you make up on the spot. If you charge too little, the event costs you money; if you charge too much without explaining what's included, the inquiry goes to the next venue. This guide is for owners of event venues, country houses and event spaces: a method for setting your rate, what to include, how to vary it by date, what to put in writing and how to answer when someone asks for a price. There are no market prices here: you set the numbers from your own costs.

## Start with your costs

The floor of your rate is what it costs you to open the venue for an event. Split it in two:

**Per-event costs** (you only have them if there's an event):

- **Cleaning**, before and after.
- **Your own staff** working that night: manager, security, maintenance.
- **Electricity, gas and water** used in those hours.
- **Supplies**: cleaning products, paper, replacing whatever gets broken.

**Monthly fixed costs** (you pay them whether there's an event or not):

- Rent or property taxes, basic utilities, subscriptions.
- **Insurance** for the venue.
- Maintenance, fixed salaries, accountant.

To know how much falls on each date, divide the month's fixed costs by the number of events you really do in a normal month, not your best one. Add that to the per-event cost and you have the minimum you need to charge so you don't lose money. Your profit goes on top.

## Choose how to charge

There are three ways, and you can combine them:

- **Per event.** One price for the date and a fixed time slot. It's the easiest to understand for weddings, quinceañeras or parties.
- **Per hour.** Works for short uses: company meetings, training sessions, video productions. Set a minimum number of hours, because setting up and cleaning take the same for two hours as for six.
- **Per head.** Works if you include services that grow with the number of guests (crockery, furniture, floor staff). Combine it with a **minimum number of people**: otherwise a small event costs you almost the same as a big one and you charge much less.

Choose one main method and always use it the same way. Two clients comparing your quotes need to be able to understand them.

## What's included and what isn't

Before putting down a number, write the list of what goes into that price:

| What | What to define |
|---|---|
| Hours | From and until what time, including setup and teardown |
| Furniture and crockery | How much is included and what happens if they ask for more |
| Sound and lighting | Whether it's the house equipment and who operates it |
| Kitchen | Whether it can be used and on what terms |
| Catering | Whether it's in-house or they can bring their own, and whether that has a cost |
| Cleaning | Whether the final clean is included |
| Staff | Manager, security, toilets |

Whatever isn't on the list is charged separately or provided by the client. If someone asks "is this included?" and the answer is "it depends", you still need to write it down. How to explain it on your listing is in [how to write an event venue listing](/en/blog/how-to-write-an-event-venue-listing).

## Vary the price by date

With the base price set, decide beforehand how it changes:

- **High and low season.** Mark on the calendar which months have the most demand in your area. If you have a country house with a pool, summer rules; if you work with companies, the end of the year. What's worth preparing for that season is in [event venues in the end-of-year season](/en/blog/event-venue-end-of-year-season).
- **Day of the week.** Saturday night isn't worth the same as Tuesday lunchtime. A different rate for low-demand days helps you fill them.
- **Time slot.** If you hire out lunchtime and evening slots, each has its own price and finish time.
- **Overtime.** A price per hour or part hour, stated beforehand, with a cut-off time that isn't exceeded because of the neighbours or your permit. If your venue is in Buenos Aires City, what the permit says is in [event venue permits in Buenos Aires City](/en/blog/event-venue-permit-in-buenos-aires-city).

Build a rate card with all of this and keep it to hand. That way you quote in five minutes and always the same way.

## Deposit and cancellation, in writing

How people book is part of the price. Put in writing, in the same email where you quote:

- **How much the deposit is**, when it's paid and from when the date is confirmed.
- **What happens if the client cancels**, and whether it changes depending on notice.
- **When the balance is paid.**
- **What's charged separately**: overtime, breakages, extra cleaning.
- **Until when the quote is valid.**

In Argentina, if you hire out to private individuals for their party, that person is a consumer under **Consumer Protection Law 24,240**. **Article 4** requires you to state accurately, clearly and in detail the characteristics of the service and the terms on which it's sold. **Article 7** says that an offer made to the public binds whoever makes it while it's valid, and that it has to state its dates, terms, conditions or limitations. Applied to a venue: whatever you publish or send as a price, you have to honour on those terms, so write down what's included and until when it's valid. Outside Argentina, the rules depend on your country.

## How to show the price on your LABURO listing

Your venue listing has no price field. What visitors do see is a note: you'll send price and availability by email, after reading the inquiry, because they depend on the date and on how many people there are.

What helps you filter inquiries without putting a number:

- **Maximum guests**, entered properly: it's the figure people search by.
- **Minimum guests**, if you charge per head or don't hire out for small groups.
- **The "what the venue has" list**, ticking only what's really included.
- **Whether outside catering can be brought in.**
- **In the description**: how you charge (per event, per hour or per head), what the base hire includes and what's charged separately.

## How to answer when someone asks for a price

With the rate card ready, the answer comes out in order:

1. **Whether the date is free** and whether the venue works for that number in that layout.
2. **The price for that date and time slot**, with the charging method.
3. **What's included and what isn't**, in the same list as always.
4. **Deposit, cancellation and quote validity.**

If you're missing a detail to quote (hours, numbers, whether they're bringing catering), ask in a single message. The rest is in [how to answer a venue inquiry](/en/blog/how-to-answer-a-venue-inquiry) and, for building the proposal, in [how to reply to an event quote request](/en/blog/how-to-reply-to-an-event-quote-request). If the person wants to see the place first, read [how to prepare an event venue tour](/en/blog/how-to-prepare-an-event-venue-tour).

## Common mistakes

- **Quoting without knowing your costs** and discovering at the event that you lost money.
- **A different price every time** for the same date and the same service.
- **Not saying what's included** and arguing about it later.
- **Charging per head with no minimum.**
- **Overtime with no price** agreed beforehand.

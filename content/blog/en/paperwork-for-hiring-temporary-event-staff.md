Before we start, one clarification that matters: **this is not legal or accounting advice**. The type of contract you use, insurance cover and tax obligations are decided by your lawyer or accountant according to your case, your jurisdiction and the type of event. Look for that answer there, not in a blog.

What follows is something else: the **minimum operational file** you should be able to open for every person who set foot in your event. It's what saves you when there's a claim, when the client audits, when the venue asks for the list or when someone disputes how many hours they worked.

## 1. Identity and age

It sounds obvious and it fails all the time: you need to know **who each person is**, with a document, not a first name and a phone number.

- Document: national ID, tax ID or passport.
- Date of birth, which is what lets you check they're of age before assigning someone to a position.
- Country and city of residence.

In LABURO these fields are filled in at sign-up and the producer sees them on the candidate's profile before sending anything. It isn't red tape: it's the difference between knowing who you're putting on the door and guessing.

## 2. The person's situation to work

This is the piece of information most often skipped, especially at events with international staff or productions that move between countries.

In the LABURO sign-up each person declares their **situation to work**: whether they're a citizen or a resident with permission, whether they have a work permit, whether they'd need a sponsor or a visa, or whether they're not sure. That last answer is useful information too: it tells you there's something to check before confirming, not after.

## 3. The conditions, in writing and accepted

An "ok, sure" in a group chat isn't paperwork. This is: a confirmation that contains **role, date, venue, hours, amount and payment terms**, and an explicit acceptance from the person.

In LABURO the offer arrives by email with its own link that expires. The person opens it, sees the role, dates, hours, amount and any particular conditions, and accepts or declines right there. Once they accept, **they're confirmed for that event**, and on the producer's side the status of that offer is visible at all times: sent, seen, accepted, declined or expired.

That solves two things at once: the person knows exactly what they committed to, and you have a record that they accepted.

The payment terms are written down too: in LABURO payment is coordinated with SOMOS DER within 10 business days of the work being done. It isn't a different verbal promise for each person.

## 4. The record of hours

This is where most claims come from, and almost always for the same reason: nobody wrote anything down.

You need to be able to answer, without arguing, **who was there, in which role and during which hours**. In LABURO that's handled with a clock-in and clock-out from the phone on the day of the event. The hours are saved and staff see them in their own history, so there aren't two versions.

If you also entered the venue address, the system stores how far from the venue each clock-in happened. It doesn't block anything: it shows you and you decide.

## 5. Personal data is someone's responsibility

A spreadsheet with the name, ID number, phone, email and address of two hundred people, forwarded by email between three suppliers, is a problem waiting to happen.

Three questions you should be able to answer:

- **Did the person give consent?** In LABURO sign-up includes an express acceptance: the person knows their data is stored to assess them for event jobs.
- **Who is responsible for the database?** It's stated: SOMOS DER.
- **How do they exercise their rights?** They can access, correct or delete their data by writing to rrhh@somosder.com.ar, under Argentina's personal data protection law (Law 25,326). And they can leave the pool in one click, without asking permission or explaining anything.

## 6. What you need to ask elsewhere

No software answers these three, and it's best to have them sorted before you sign:

1. **Your legal or accounting adviser:** what type of contract you use for each person and what cover applies under it. It changes with the role, the duration and the jurisdiction.
2. **The venue:** what it requires before letting staff in. Many ask for a named list in advance, and that request has a deadline.
3. **Your client:** what paperwork they'll ask you for before releasing payment. Finding out after the event is the worst version.

## The minimum file, per person

- ID document and date of birth.
- Declared situation to work.
- Verified email and phone.
- Assigned role.
- Confirmation with conditions, accepted by the person and dated.
- Recorded clock-in and clock-out times.
- Consent for data processing.

If you can't open that file for someone, that person shouldn't be on your schedule.

This is the file [SOMOS DER](https://somosder.com) puts together before each event. It isn't theoretical advice: it's what you need to be able to show if there's an inspection or if someone gets hurt at work.

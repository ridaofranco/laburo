An outdoor festival has everything that's hard about a big event, plus the weather: many hours, a large site, sun or rain and a team spread over points that can't see each other. This guide covers the positions you almost always need, how to build shifts and what to plan so the team makes it to the end in one piece.

## The positions that are almost never missing

The list comes from the site map. Every gate, every bar and every area is a position someone has to cover for all the hours it's open.

- **Access control and registration.** Ticket scanning, wristbands, accreditation for press, artists and suppliers. It's the audience's first contact and the entry peak is the tensest moment. If there are also admission and stay control staff, the Argentine national law we explain in [the guide to Law 26,370 for door staff](/en/blog/argentina-law-26370-door-staff) applies.
- **Bars.** Bartenders and barbacks, with constant restocking of ice, cups and drinks. On a big site, restocking is what decides whether the bar sells or waits. How to work out how many you need is in [hiring bartenders for a corporate event](/en/blog/hiring-bartenders-for-a-corporate-event).
- **Cleaning.** Before, during and after. At a festival, "during" weighs most: bins that fill up, toilets, food areas. The detail is in [cleaning staff for an event](/en/blog/cleaning-staff-for-an-event).
- **Runners.** They fetch and carry whatever is missing between areas: supplies, radios, change, a cable. On a large site they're what stops someone abandoning their post to go and get something. See [what does an event runner do](/en/blog/what-does-an-event-runner-do).
- **Setup and teardown.** It starts days or hours before opening and ends when the last truck leaves. It's physical work out in the open. How to look after yourself is in [event setup and teardown: how to work safely](/en/blog/event-setup-and-teardown-work-safely).

## Long shifts: the schedule by position

A festival can have twelve hours of open gates plus setup and teardown. Nobody performs the same in hour eleven as in hour one, and a post left empty at the handover is a post that fails.

1. **Build the schedule by position, not by person.** First how many positions are open in each time slot; then who covers them.
2. **Overlap the handovers.** The incoming person arrives before the outgoing one leaves, to be told what's happened.
3. **Separate call time from opening time.** The team has to be at their posts before the public comes in, not when the gates open.
4. **Nobody works a double shift** unless it's been planned and agreed.

All of this is developed in [shift planning for long events](/en/blog/shift-planning-for-long-events).

## Heat: water, shade and breaks

At a daytime festival, the sun is a risk for the team, not just the audience. What to sort out beforehand:

- **Water for staff** throughout the day, at their own point, separate from the public bars. Nobody should have to buy water to work.
- **Shade at every fixed post.** Access points and bars are usually in the sun: a gazebo or shade cloth changes the day.
- **Breaks and rotation.** The most exposed posts rotate with more sheltered ones.
- **Clothing and protection.** If the organisers provide the T-shirt, it should be light and light-coloured. Say in the call that people should bring a cap and sunscreen.

Argentina's Ministry of Health recommends avoiding direct sun between 10 a.m. and 4 p.m., wearing loose, light, light-coloured clothing, and not drinking alcohol in the heat because it raises body temperature and fluid loss. It also lists the warning signs of heatstroke: a temperature of 39 °C or more, red, hot and dry skin, rapid breathing and pulse, a severe headache, confusion or loss of consciousness. Whoever coordinates has to know them and where the site's medical service is.

## Rain: decide it beforehand

If it rains, the festival isn't always called off, but the work changes. Decide beforehand, in writing:

- **Which posts are covered or moved** and where things that can't get wet are protected (registration equipment, tills, phones).
- **Where the team shelters** during breaks.
- **What gets reinforced afterwards.** Mud and water multiply the cleaning, and access gets slower.
- **What to bring.** If the event is in the open, the call should say so: raincoat, closed shoes, a change of clothes.

## Meeting point and communication

On a big site people get lost, phones run out of battery and the signal gets overloaded when the crowd arrives.

- **A fixed staff meeting point**, marked on the map and easy to explain. That's where people clock in, rest, drink water and go back to if something fails.
- **One lead per area**, with a name and phone number everyone knows before starting.
- **A channel that doesn't rely only on phones.** If there are radios, who has them; if not, a fixed time to stop by the meeting point.
- **A plan if someone is missing.** Which position is left uncovered and who covers it. For that, see [event staff no-show: what to do](/en/blog/event-staff-no-show-what-to-do).

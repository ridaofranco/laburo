In almost every event team there is one person the rest look at when something isn't clear. They don't always have the title, but they already do part of the job: they organise, explain and keep people informed. If that person is you, the next step is coordinating. This guide explains what a staff coordinator does, what sets them apart, how to tell you're ready and how to make the jump without your first event in charge becoming a bad memory.

## What a staff coordinator does

The coordinator doesn't do the work of each position: they make sure every position is covered and running. Their day starts earlier and ends later than everyone else's.

- **They call people in.** They confirm who's coming, at what time and to which position, and confirm again the day before. A call with no reply is a possible gap, not a yes.
- **They give the briefing.** Before doors open they gather the team and explain in a few minutes what the event is, who the client is as far as the staff need to know, which position each person has, who to ask and what not to do.
- **They build the shift and break schedule.** Who is in each position, when they go for a meal and who covers them in the meantime. The full logic is in [shift planning for long events](/en/blog/shift-planning-for-long-events).
- **They track attendance.** They know at all times who has arrived, who is at their position and who has left. If the event uses clock-in, they check it; if not, they keep their own list.
- **They handle dropouts.** Someone doesn't arrive, someone feels unwell, someone has to leave early. The coordinator rearranges the schedule and tells production if a replacement is needed. For that moment, read [event staff no-show: what to do](/en/blog/event-staff-no-show-what-to-do).
- **They are the link with production.** Staff don't go to the producer with every question: they go to the coordinator, who filters, solves what they can and passes up what they can't.
- **They close the day.** They check that equipment and credentials have been returned and that nobody left without saying, and they send production a short report: what went well, what failed, who stood out.

## The skills that set them apart

Being good at your position is the base, but it isn't enough. What separates good staff from a good coordinator is something else:

- **Seeing the whole event.** The coordinator knows where the bar, the door, the toilets, the storeroom and the emergency exit are, and when each place fills up.
- **Giving short, clear instructions.** "You're on door two until ten, then she covers you" works better than a long explanation.
- **Saying no without a fight.** To a colleague who wants to leave early, to a guest asking for something outside the plan, sometimes to the client.
- **Staying calm when something breaks.** The team copies the coordinator's tone. If the coordinator runs, everyone runs.
- **Reporting without being asked.** Production wants to hear about things before they discover them.

## Signs you're ready

- New colleagues ask you, not the producer.
- Production asks you to flag when something is missing or to explain a position to someone.
- You know how the event unfolds, not just your own hours.
- You solve small problems without escalating each one.
- You have a record with no no-shows and no late arrivals. Without that, no production company will put people in your charge.

If you recognise yourself in most of these, you're already doing part of the coordination work. What's missing is someone giving it to you formally.

## How to earn a production company's trust

A production company hands a team to someone who has already shown they don't need supervision. That's built with three things, event after event:

1. **Punctuality.** Arriving at the call time, which is not the opening time. The coordinator arrives even before everyone else.
2. **Reporting.** Saying what's happening (good and bad) without waiting to be asked. A short message at the end of the day with the news goes a long way.
3. **Solving.** When a problem comes up, bringing the problem and a proposal, not just the problem.

The rest is in [how to get regular event work](/en/blog/how-to-get-regular-event-work): the behaviours that get you called back are the same ones that get you put in charge of people.

## First steps

You don't need to jump straight into coordinating a whole event. There are two safer routes:

- **Lead a small section.** A bar, a door, the cloakroom, a group of promo staff. A few people, one space and one type of task. That's where you learn to cover breaks, give instructions and report.
- **Be a coordinator's second.** You watch how they build the schedule, give the briefing and handle dropouts, and they gradually hand you parts of the job. It's the fastest way to learn without carrying everything.

If your path leads towards production and the stage, the next steps are other positions with their own names, like the one explained in [what is a stage manager at an event](/en/blog/what-is-a-stage-manager-at-an-event).

## Mistakes on your first event as coordinator

- **Carrying on as staff.** Serving or scanning and losing sight of the team. Helping for a while is fine; staying in one position isn't.
- **Giving the briefing from memory.** Bring it written down, even on your phone: positions, times, contacts, rules.
- **Not planning breaks.** If nobody knows when each person eats, they all go at once or nobody goes.
- **Not having a replacement in mind.** Before doors open, know who can move positions if someone is missing.
- **Being hard to find.** The team and production need to know where you are and how to reach you.
- **Leaving without closing.** The day ends when everything has been returned and production has the report, not when the last guest leaves.

The LABURO role catalogue has three coordination positions: **Logistics coordinator** (in Production and logistics), **Guest coordinator** (in Service and experience) and **Volunteer coordinator** (in Other profiles).

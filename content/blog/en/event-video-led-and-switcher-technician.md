At many events, what the audience looks at most is not the stage but the screen. That is where the speaker appears in close-up, along with the presentation, the brand video, the countdown. Behind all that are two trades: the person who sets up and looks after the image (video or LED technician) and the person who decides which image goes out at each moment (switcher operator). This guide is for anyone who wants to do that work: what each one does, how to get in and how to set up your profile so the offer reaches you.

If your thing is sound or lighting, that is in [event sound and lighting technician jobs](/en/blog/event-sound-and-lighting-technician-jobs). Here we stick to video.

## Two roles, one team

At LABURO, the **technical (sound, lighting, video)** category has two image roles:

- **Video and LED technician.** Looks after the kit: LED screens, projectors, confidence monitors for the speaker, processors, cables and connections. Their question is "does it look right and is the signal getting there?".
- **Switcher operator.** Looks after the decision: which source goes to the screen (or the stream) at each moment and when it changes. Their question is "what does the audience need to see now?".

At a small event, one person may do both. At a large one they are separate roles, and the switcher operator works alongside camera operators, graphics and, if there is a stream, whoever handles the streaming.

## A video or LED technician's day

**Setup.** An event LED screen is usually built from panels that slot together on a frame, either flown or ground-stacked. The cases are unloaded, the frame is built with the setup and rigging crews, the panels are placed in order and data and power are connected. It is physical work, sometimes at height: if you haven't trained to fly structures, the rigger does that. How to look after yourself at this stage is in [event setup and teardown: how to work safely](/en/blog/event-setup-and-teardown-work-safely).

**Configuration.** With the screen on, you check that no panels are dead or showing a different colour, adjust the brightness to the venue (a dark function room is not the same as an outdoor site in full sun) and configure the processor so the image fits completely without distortion. If there are projectors, they are aligned and focused.

**Testing sources.** Everything that is going to go out gets connected: the presentation laptop, the videos, the cameras, the graphics. You test with the event's real material, not a sample image. This is the moment to discover that the video is in the wrong format or that the speaker's laptop isn't sending a signal, not when they are on stage.

**During the event.** You stay close to the kit, alert. If a panel fails, a signal drops or a confidence monitor goes off, you fix it without the audience finding out.

**Load-out.** Everything is disconnected, taken down panel by panel and each item goes back in its case. Panels are expensive and fragile at the edges: rushing here is what costs the most.

## A switcher operator's day

**Before.** You read the event script or running order: who speaks, in what order, when each video plays, which graphic goes with each speaker. You set up the sources on the switcher and, if needed, the layouts (for example, the speaker on one side and the presentation on the other).

**At rehearsal.** The event is run through with whoever is directing and with the [stage manager](/en/blog/what-is-a-stage-manager-at-an-event), who usually calls the cues. This is where you agree how changes will be signalled to you: by intercom, by script or by hand signals.

**Live.** You follow the script and the cues, and make each change at exactly the right moment. You talk to the camera operators to ask for the shot you need. If something isn't ready (a video that won't load, a badly framed camera), you always have a safe image to show while it gets sorted.

**What's valued most:** concentration from start to finish, calm and anticipation. A good operator already has the next image ready before it is asked for.

## How to get into the trade

The paths that come up most often:

1. **As an assistant at screen or projection rental companies.** You prep kit, load, assemble panels and take down. It is the most common school for LED technicians.
2. **At technical or audiovisual production companies.** There you are close to the operating and the switcher, and you can move from assistant to operating at small events.
3. **From audiovisual work.** Many people who do camera work, editing or streaming end up operating a switcher, because they already understand shots and signals. If you come from there, also see [event photographer and videographer work](/en/blog/event-photographer-and-videographer-work).
4. **Training separately.** A video or broadcasting course helps you understand what you see, but you learn event kit by setting it up.

## What's expected

- **Punctuality.** Setting up a screen starts many hours before the event.
- **Saying what you don't know.** If you have never used that processor or that switcher, say so beforehand. Nobody fires you for asking; they will for leaving the screen black.
- **Order.** Labelled cables, numbered panels, closed cases. The load-out shows who is tidy.
- **Calm when live.** When something fails, the audience mustn't find out.
- **Staying until the end.** The load-out is part of the job and has to be in the hours on the offer.

Before accepting, make sure these are clear: full hours including setup and load-out, what kit you'll be operating, whether you bring your own tools, and the pay.

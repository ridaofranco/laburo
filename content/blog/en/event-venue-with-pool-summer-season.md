In summer, a country house (what Argentines call a *quinta*) or a venue with a garden and pool has something an indoor venue doesn't: the outdoors. But the outdoors brings questions an indoor venue never gets: what happens if it rains, where the shade is, whether the pool can be used, where people park and how late music can play. This guide is for owners and managers of country houses and venues with outdoor space who want to reach the season with those answers ready.

## Show the outdoors as it really is

People looking for a country house for summer decide a lot based on photos. The ones that work best are those that look like the event the person is imagining:

- **The garden and pool at event time.** If most of your events are in the afternoon, take photos in the afternoon. A midday shot with a perfect sky doesn't show what it looks like at 7 p.m.
- **The shade that exists.** Trees, a veranda, a pergola, a gazebo. If there's little shade, better for it to show in the photo than for people to discover it on the visit.
- **The covered area.** It's the most reassuring photo, because it answers the rain question before anyone asks it.
- **A photo set up for an event**, with tables or a lounge area, if you have one from a real event.

## Rain plan: in writing before the booking

The question "what if it rains?" always comes. The answer has to be specific:

- **How many people fit under cover**, and in what layout. 100 people seated is not the same as 100 standing. If the covered area isn't enough for the whole event, say so before the booking.
- **What gets moved and who moves it.** Tables, bar, sound. If everything has to go inside, someone has to do it and that takes time.
- **The decision deadline.** Agree a cut-off time with the client to choose between setting up outside or inside, so suppliers don't set up twice.
- **Whether a marquee is an option**, who hires it and who pays for it. Don't promise a marquee you don't have.

If the outdoors is half of your offer, the rain plan is the other half. Put it in writing in the proposal, along with what the hire includes.

## Shade, heat and hours

In summer, the sunniest hours are the hardest for being outside, for guests and for the team working. A few things help:

- **Offer slots that make the most of the afternoon and sunset**, and say which parts of the grounds are shaded at each time.
- **Think about where the bar and catering go.** In direct sun, food and drink get complicated. If you have an area with permanent shade, it's the natural place for the bar.
- **Water and toilets close by.** The more people outside and the hotter it is, the more they get used.

The event team also works outdoors, often for many hours. If whoever hires your venue asks for advice on staffing, [outdoor festival staffing](/en/blog/outdoor-festival-staffing) has points that work for a country house too.

## Pool rules, always in writing

The pool is the most asked-about item and the one argued about most afterwards. You don't need to invent rules: you need yours to be written down and for the client to read them before paying the deposit. The minimum:

- **Whether the pool can be used or is decorative only** during the event.
- **During what hours** it can be used, if it can.
- **If there will be children**, who is responsible for watching them. This applies above all to birthdays and quinceañera parties.
- **What happens with glass** near the water, whether you allow it or not.

If your local or regional authority has rules on pools at properties hired out for events, check them at its official source before promising anything. We don't give rules in this guide because they change from place to place.

## Parking and access

Country houses tend to be far from public transport, so almost everyone arrives by car or shuttle. Explain:

- **How many cars fit** inside the property, if any, and what happens with the rest.
- **Where suppliers come in** and what time they can arrive to set up.
- **What the road is like**, especially if there are dirt stretches that get tricky in the rain.
- **The exact address and directions**, to send in a single message to whoever books.

Think about the trip home too. An event that ends late at a country house leaves guests and staff far from home: [getting home after a late-night event](/en/blog/getting-home-after-a-late-night-event) gives the point of view of the people working.

## Neighbours and noise

The outdoors is also what the neighbours hear most. Before the season, be clear and put in writing how late music can play outside, whether it has to move indoors after a certain time and what volume you accept. If your property is in an area with its own rules (a gated community, for example), the community's conditions also go into the proposal.

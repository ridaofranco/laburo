To find event work today you almost always have to hand over your details somewhere: an app, a form, a WhatsApp group, a spreadsheet someone shares. You are often asked for your name, phone number, ID, photos and where you live before anyone tells you what the job is. This guide is a list of questions to ask yourself before giving those details, whether it's LABURO or any other platform. At the end we explain how LABURO handles it, with what can be checked on its pages.

## Who is behind it

The first thing is to know who you are giving your details to. A serious platform says who runs it and how to contact them: a named company, an email address that answers, a page with its rules. If it's a group or a social media profile, ask which production company or agency they work for and look it up yourself.

Be wary if:

- There is no company name and no way of contacting them other than a lone phone number.
- The person recruiting can't tell you who the event is for or where it is.
- They rush you to send everything "today, because places are filling up".

## Whether it has a privacy policy, and what it says

In Argentina, Law 25,326 on Personal Data Protection says that when your data is requested you must be told, beforehand and clearly, what it will be used for, who is responsible for the database, whether answering is compulsory or not, and that you can exercise your rights of access, correction and deletion. A privacy policy is where a platform explains all of that.

When you read it, look for concrete answers:

- **Which data it keeps** and what it uses it for.
- **Who sees your contact details:** whether your phone number is visible to anyone or only to whoever is going to hire you.
- **Who it shares them with** and whether it sells them.
- **How long** it keeps them and how you delete them.

If the policy is a generic text that mentions none of these things, take note.

## What data they ask for, and why

For people to find you for an event, they need your name, a contact, your area, your trades and your experience. ID and date of birth are sometimes requested for hiring. What makes no sense is being asked for account passwords, your card details or codes you receive by text message: nobody needs those to give you a job.

The Argentine law also singles out sensitive data (racial or ethnic origin, political opinions, religious beliefs, trade union membership, health or sex life). If a form to work as a promoter or a waiter asks about any of those, ask why.

## Whether they charge you to apply

This is the clearest warning sign. Being asked for money to sign up, to "reserve your place", for a compulsory course before you can work or for the uniform before your first event is not normal. The person who works is the one who gets paid.

Also be careful with offers that don't say how much they pay. A real offer tells you the role, the date, the hours, the venue and the pay before you say yes. If the pay shows up as "to be agreed" until the day of the event, ask beforehand. The basics of how that gets arranged are in [what event work is like and how you get paid](/en/blog/what-event-work-is-like-and-pay).

## Whether you can leave and delete your data

Before you go in, check how you get out. In Argentina, Law 25,326 gives you the right to ask which of your data they hold (whoever is responsible has ten calendar days to answer) and to ask for it to be corrected, updated or deleted, within a maximum of five working days. Correcting and deleting inaccurate or incomplete data is free. If they don't answer, the law allows a habeas data action in court.

In practice, a platform that takes this seriously tells you on its pages who to write to if you want to leave, with no hidden forms.

At a conference, a gala or a concert, the audience sees a speaker who walks on just as the video ends, a band that starts as the lights go down and a finale that arrives on time. Behind it there's a person at the side of the stage, with a radio and a running order, who said "go" at every moment. That person is the stage manager. This guide explains what they do, how the role differs from other production positions and how you become one.

## What a stage manager does

The stage manager is responsible for what happens on stage going in the order, at the time and in the way it was planned. They don't decide the programme, but they carry it out, and they coordinate everyone who touches the stage: technical crew, speakers, artists, presenters and stage staff.

Their central tool is the **running order** (also called the rundown or technical script): the document that says, block by block, what happens, who's on stage, what plays, what's on screen, what lighting is used and how long each thing lasts.

### Before the event

- **Build or review the running order** with production: times, blocks, entrances and exits, videos, music, each speaker's microphone.
- **Know the stage:** access points, stairs, wings, where each person waits before going on and where they exit.
- **Coordinate with the technical team:** what each block needs from sound, lighting and video, and how cues will be given.
- **Run the rehearsal or technical run-through:** test entrances, scene changes and videos with the real people, if possible.

### During the show or conference

- **Call each person in time:** the next speaker has to be in the wings before they're introduced, not coming back from the toilet.
- **Give cues over the radio:** tell sound, lighting and video the exact moment of each change ("video go", "house lights down", "mic two open").
- **Keep track of time:** if a speaker runs over, the stage manager knows first and tells production which block is shortened or moved.
- **Handle what changes:** a video that won't start, an artist arriving late, a speaker asking for different music. Decisions are made fast and everyone is told on the same channel.
- **Look after the stage:** making sure nobody goes up who shouldn't and that set changes happen with the right lighting.

### After

- **Organise the exit** of artists and speakers, and hand the stage over to the technical team for teardown.
- **Pass on notes to production:** what ran late, what failed, what needs changing for the next day if the event continues.

## Communicating by radio

The radio is the stage manager's working tool. At a large event there's usually more than one channel (technical, production, security), and knowing how to use it is part of the job:

- **Short, clear messages:** first who you're talking to, then what they have to do.
- **Confirm what was asked:** a "copy" stops a cue getting lost.
- **Give a standby:** "stand by, video in one minute" and then "go". The technical team needs that time to be ready.

## How it differs from the producer and the floor coordinator

Names change from one production company to another, but the most common division is this:

| Position | Responsible for |
|---|---|
| Producer | What is done, with whom and with what resources. Client, budget, hiring, programme. |
| Stage manager | Making sure the programme is carried out on stage: running order, cues, entrances, timing. |
| Floor coordinator | The hall and the people: staff, areas, circulation, what happens off the stage. |

At small events these functions merge, and the stage manager may also be the floor coordinator. In LABURO, besides Stage manager, the same family includes Régisseur, a name some production companies use for similar tasks: ask what it includes before accepting.

## The skills required

- **Calm under pressure.** The show doesn't stop, and changes are solved in seconds.
- **Organisation and reading the running order,** with the ability to redo it on the spot if something moves.
- **Basic technical knowledge:** you don't need to operate a desk, but you do need to understand what sound, lighting and video need for each change.
- **Dealing with speakers and artists.** You have to tell someone their time is up or that they need to wait, without starting a conflict.
- **Languages,** if the event hosts speakers or artists from abroad.

## How you become a stage manager

You almost never start there: you get there after seeing many shows up close. The most common routes:

1. **From production assistant.** You learn to build schedules, deal with suppliers and understand how a programme is decided. The natural step is asking to be put at the side of the stage.
2. **From stage runner or assistant stage manager.** You bring the speakers, find the artists, update the running order.
3. **From technical work.** Anyone coming from stagehand, backline, sound or lighting already knows the stage and the language of cues. They lack the dealings with talent and the production view, but they start with an advantage.

In every case, what brings you closer to the role is asking to be at rehearsals, watching how each event's stage manager works and offering to do stage tasks.

## How to build your profile so you get called

People looking for a stage manager aren't looking for just anyone: they're looking for someone who has already been on a stage. Your profile has to show that.

- **Mark Stage manager and also the positions you already do** (production assistant, stagehand, backline). That way you get called for those while you build experience in the other.
- **Describe your experience specifically:** what type of event (conference, show, gala), what size and what you did. "Assisted the stage manager at a two-day conference with twenty speakers" says much more than "production experience".
- **Add the link to your portfolio or LinkedIn,** and if you have running orders you built (without client details), show them.
- **Set your area and availability properly,** since they're the filters people use to find you.

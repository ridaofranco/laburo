At a conference or a corporate event, the first impression doesn't come from the stage: it comes from the registration desk. If the queue moves, the event starts well. If it gets stuck, the first talk starts with half the room empty. This guide explains what registration (check-in) staff do, what profile is wanted and how the work is organised so opening doesn't become a bottleneck.

## What registration staff do

Registration staff confirm that whoever arrives is registered, validate their access and hand over the badge for their category. At the door you get attendees with QR codes, speakers in a hurry, sponsors coming in early to set up their stand, press, last-minute guests and people who registered with another email.

That's why the work divides into three moments.

### Before opening

- **Arrive before anyone else.** Registration staff are usually called in well ahead of doors opening to set up the desk.
- **Set up the desk:** devices charged, connection tested, badges sorted, signage for each queue.
- **Do the trial run:** scan or look up several real people from the list and check the system responds properly.
- **Go over the categories** and which areas each one can enter, and know who to call with a question (coordination, the client's organisers).
- **Check in those who enter early:** exhibitors, sponsors, technical crew and in-house staff.

### During the rush

- **Receive, validate and hand over** without chatting too much: a short greeting, scan or search, badge, a direction for where to go.
- **Send exceptions** to the right desk, instead of solving them in the main queue.
- **Tell coordination** if the queue grows faster than it shrinks, so people can be moved from another post.
- **Protect the data:** the list has names, companies and emails. Don't leave the screen visible or show the list to anyone.

### After opening

- **Keep a reduced desk** for late arrivals or people coming between sessions.
- **Reprint or replace** lost badges.
- **Close the desk:** return equipment and give coordination the day's notes (people not on the list, system problems, complaints).
- At multi-day events, **day two is different:** most people already have their badge and the work becomes control and exceptions.

## The profile asked for

- **A friendly, firm manner.** Registration staff say no many times a day without starting an argument.
- **Speed and accuracy** to find a name, read an ID or spot a wrong category.
- **Comfort with phones, tablets or computers,** because almost all registration systems are digital.
- **Calm at the rush.** The queue puts on pressure, and the typical rush mistake is hurrying and handing over the wrong badge.
- **Languages,** if the conference hosts people from abroad: English, and sometimes Portuguese.

If you want to do this work, experience in reception, customer service or sales counts a lot. [What event work is like and how you get paid](/en/blog/what-event-work-is-like-and-pay) covers the basics of the trade.

## How desks are organised at the arrival rush

Most conference attendees arrive in the hour before the first talk. That peak, not the total number of registrations, is what determines how many desks you need. The key is to separate tasks:

1. **Scanning desks** for people with their QR code or on the list. This is the fast lane.
2. **An exceptions desk:** the person who didn't register, who used another email, whose phone died. If these cases are handled in the fast lane, they hold up the whole queue.
3. **A printing and hand-out desk,** if badges are printed. The printer sets its own pace, and it's best not handled by the same person who scans.
4. **A desk for special categories** (speakers, press, sponsors), so they don't wait in the general queue.
5. **One person in the queue,** who keeps it orderly, asks people before they reach the desk whether they have their QR code ready and sends exceptions to the right place.

### How to measure the pace instead of guessing

There's no number of check-ins per minute that works for every event: it depends on the system, whether badges are printed, how many exceptions come up and each person's experience. What you can do is measure it:

- At the trial run, **time several real check-ins** from start to finish (from when the person reaches the desk until they leave with the badge) and take the average.
- **Separate the times:** one for people with a QR code, another for an exception. They're usually very different.
- With the pace per desk and the number of people you expect in the peak hour, you calculate how many desks you need so the wait doesn't exceed what you want. PASE has an access point calculator that does this maths and uses paces measured at real events as a starting point.

The full calculation, desk by desk, is in [how many staff for an event](/en/blog/how-many-staff-for-an-event).

## Common mistakes

- **Mixing exceptions with the fast lane.** A single complicated case holds up everyone behind.
- **Skipping the trial run.** The connection or printer problem appears exactly when the doors open.
- **Calling staff in at opening time.** Registration staff have to have the desk set up before the first attendee arrives.
- **Sizing by registrations instead of the peak.** A thousand registrations spread over the day aren't the same as a thousand in one hour.
- **Not defining who decides.** When a guest isn't on the list, registration staff need to know who to ask, not improvise.

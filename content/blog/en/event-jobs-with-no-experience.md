Almost everyone who works at events today had a first event with no experience. The question isn't whether you can get in without having worked before, but where to start.

This guide goes in order: which positions take people with no experience, what to put in your profile, how to build references from scratch and what to do on your first day.

## Where to start: entry-level positions

At LABURO, positions are organised in families (bar and catering, access and check-in, production and logistics, technical, audiovisual, guest experience, sales, cleaning, health and safety, transport). In each one there are roles that need a trade and roles you learn on the job.

The ones most often used as a way in:

| Family | Entry-level positions | What counts |
|---|---|---|
| Bar and catering | Barback, runner, dishwasher, kitchen assistant | Pace, tidiness, staying on your feet |
| Access and check-in | Cloakroom, wristbands and badges, usher | Dealing with people, attention to detail |
| Production and logistics | Load-in and load-out, setup and teardown | Strength, punctuality, following instructions |
| Guest experience | Promoter, sampling, host | Presence, good communication |
| Sales | Merchandise sales | Basic till handling and dealing with the public |
| Cleaning and maintenance | Cleaning staff, restrooms | Consistency through the whole shift |

What you shouldn't tick if you've never done it: roles with technical or safety responsibility (rigger, electrician, sound or lighting technician, first aid, lifeguard, doctor or nurse). The trade, the certification or the licence can't be learned overnight, and whoever hires knows it.

## What to put in your profile when you have no experience

On the other side, whoever builds the team filters by role, area and availability. With no experience, those three fields matter even more, because they're all they have to decide.

- **A few positions, well chosen.** Tick the entry-level ones you'd really do. Twenty trades ticked put you in searches where you don't fit.
- **City and region.** Many searches are by area, precisely so they don't call someone who lives two hours from the venue.
- **Availability.** Weekends, willing to travel, own transport. Most events are at night and at weekends: if you're available, say so.
- **How much notice you need.** If you can start the next day, say it. When someone has to cover a spot fast, this field gets checked.
- **Previous experience outside events.** Write what you have done: "One year serving at a fast-food counter, till and order prep" says far more than "great attitude".
- **Email and phone written correctly.** Offers often arrive by email and links expire. Check your spam folder during the first days.

## How to build references from scratch

A reference is someone who can say "this person delivers". They don't need to be from the events world.

**Before your first event.** Think about who has seen you work: the manager of a bar or a shop, a warehouse supervisor, someone you did deliveries or customer service for. If the job looks like the position you're after, that reference counts. Ask for permission before giving it, and tell them what kind of job it's for. A reference who doesn't know they'll be called helps nobody.

**Something formal that sets you apart.** If you're going into catering, a food handler certificate (the name and requirements depend on your country or city) is a concrete fact you can add to your experience. It doesn't replace experience, but it shows you take the job seriously.

**After your first event.** That's where the reference that counts most begins: your own. The jobs you've done build up, and the person who coordinated you that day is your next reference in the trade. When you finish, ask whether you can mention them in the future.

What you shouldn't do: invent events you didn't work. In this trade people know each other, and a lie in your profile gets found out on the first shift.

## Before you accept: read the offer

An offer should include the role, the event, the date, the venue, the hours and the pay. Three things worth checking before you say yes:

1. **The call time**, which is almost never the time the event opens.
2. **The conditions**: dress code, footwear, whether you need to bring anything.
3. **How you get home.** A teardown that ends in the early hours in an area with no transport gets sorted out beforehand, not at 5 in the morning.

If you can't make it, decline: that way they can find someone else in time.

## Your first day: what gets you a second one

Your first event isn't won by knowing everything. It's won with simple things:

- **Arrive at the call time**, not a few minutes later.
- **Ask early** who your point of contact is, what exactly your position is and how breaks work.
- **Clock in and out** if the event uses a check-in system. It's what records your hours.
- **Let them know as soon as you know you can't go.** A warning hours ahead can be covered; a missing person discovered with the doors open can't.

An event at a hotel has an advantage and a trap. The advantage: there's a kitchen, function rooms, toilets, in-house staff and someone who knows the building. The trap: the hotel is a house with its own rules and guests, and if it isn't clear who does what, on the day you get gaps (nobody at registration) or clashes (your waiters and the hotel's in the same room). This guide sorts out what the hotel usually provides, what the production company usually brings and how to coordinate so there are no surprises.

## What the hotel usually provides

Every hotel puts its proposal together differently, so what follows is the usual pattern, not a rule. What counts is what the events department puts in writing.

- **Banqueting.** Kitchen, waiters, floor manager or maître d', table setup and linen. It's the heart of the hotel's service and almost always done by its own staff.
- **Drinks.** The bar is often the hotel's too. If you want your own bar or outside bartenders, ask beforehand: it's one of the first things to be restricted.
- **Building security.** The hotel looks after its building and its guests. That isn't the same as access control to your function room: the hotel's security person won't check your guest list.
- **Basic tech.** Some hotels have their own sound and projection, or a tech supplier that works in-house. Ask whether outside tech can be brought in and under what conditions.
- **Maintenance and cleaning** of common areas.

## What the production company usually brings

Everything that belongs to the event and not to the building:

- **Registration.** Registration desk, lists, badges and control of who goes into the room. It's what most often gets forgotten, because people assume "the hotel does it". To size it, see [what conference registration staff do](/en/blog/conference-registration-staff-role).
- **Hostesses and reception.** They welcome guests, direct them within the hotel (which usually has several floors and rooms) and escort special guests. What's required is in [corporate event hostess and reception](/en/blog/corporate-event-hostess-and-reception).
- **Runners.** In a hotel the distances are long: from the room to the storeroom, from the lobby to backstage, from one floor to another. A runner saves the coordinator from leaving their post. More in [what does an event runner do](/en/blog/what-does-an-event-runner-do).
- **Your own tech or the hired supplier's**, if the hotel allows it, with their setup crew.
- **Coordination.** One person from the production company who talks to the hotel's events department throughout the event.
- **Promo or brand staff**, if there are stands or activations.

## How to coordinate with the hotel's events department

The events (or banqueting) department is your point of contact. Before calling in a single person, agree with them:

1. **Who does what, in writing.** A simple two-column list: what the hotel provides and what the production company provides. Anything in neither column is a gap.
2. **A contact for the day.** Not the sales person who sold you the room: the person who'll be at the hotel that day, with their phone number.
3. **The hotel's times.** From when you can come in to set up, by when you have to clear out and whether there's another event before or after in the same room.
4. **How many outside people will come in.** The hotel usually asks for a list with the names and ID numbers of staff and suppliers.
5. **Where your team eats and changes.** Not all hotels offer meals or changing rooms to outside staff. If they don't, tell your people beforehand.

## Overnight setups

In hotels it's common to set up at night or in the early hours, when the room is freed up from another event. Three things to bear in mind:

- **Noise.** There are guests asleep. Ask what can be done at that hour (screwing, moving structures, sound checks) and what has to wait until morning.
- **Shifts.** An overnight setup followed by the event is a long day for anyone working both. Keep the setup team separate from the event team when you can. The shift logic is in [shift planning for long events](/en/blog/shift-planning-for-long-events), and looking after your body in [event setup and teardown: how to work safely](/en/blog/event-setup-and-teardown-work-safely).
- **Getting home.** If someone finishes at 4 a.m., the time has to be in the offer and the trip home has to be planned.

## Service access

Staff and suppliers almost never come in through the lobby. Before the event, each person needs to know:

- **Where to come in** (service door, loading bay, car park) and whether they have to check in at a post.
- **What to identify themselves with:** ID, the production company's badge or the list sent to the hotel.
- **Which goods lift or service lift to use** and when it's available.
- **Where to leave their things** during the event.

That text, short and with a map if possible, is sent together with the confirmation.

## House rules

Every hotel has its own, and it's worth asking for them in writing to pass on to the team. What usually comes up: don't use the guest lifts, don't walk through the room corridors, don't smoke where it isn't allowed, don't stick anything on the walls without permission and don't consume what belongs to the banqueting service. There are often dress rules for anyone in view as well. How to communicate that is in [event staff dress code](/en/blog/event-staff-dress-code).

## If you're working an event at a hotel

- **Come in through the entrance you were told to use**, not the lobby.
- **Have your ID to hand:** you'll probably be looked up on a list.
- **Respect the hotel's spaces:** you're a guest in a house that keeps running while you work.
- **Ask your coordinator, not the hotel staff**, unless you're told otherwise.

At a wedding or a gala dinner, guests see waiters coming and going in sync, plates arriving hot at every table at once and a toast that starts just as everyone has a full glass. Behind that there is one person setting the pace: the maître d' or floor manager. This guide is for people who work at events and want to reach that role: what it coordinates, how to get there from waiter and how to add it to your profile.

If you are still on the floor with a tray, the basics of the trade (course-by-course service, plating, cocktail service) are in [event waiter jobs](/en/blog/event-waiter-jobs). Here we pick up where that guide ends.

## What a maître d' does at an event

In a restaurant the maître d' greets, seats and looks after a room they know. At an event the job is different: **building and running the service for a single night**, with a fixed menu, a room that may be new to you and a team that often meets that same day.

What's in your hands:

- **Dividing up the room.** Which tables each waiter takes, who covers the top table, who stays on support.
- **The timing of each course.** You give the signal to the kitchen and the floor. Nobody goes out on their own.
- **The bridge with the kitchen and the bar.** If the kitchen falls behind, you know before anyone else and you rearrange.
- **Dietary restrictions.** Gluten-free, vegetarian, allergies: you check that each plate reaches the right chair.
- **Whatever happens at the table.** A broken glass, a guest asking for something off the menu, an extra chair. You solve it without the rest noticing.

## What you coordinate at a wedding

A wedding has a script and the service has to follow it. Before the event you should have:

- **The seating plan**, with the top table and the restrictions marked.
- **The minute-by-minute schedule**: drinks reception, the couple's entrance, each course, first dance, party, dessert table, close.
- **What the venue brings and what the caterer brings**, so you know who replaces crockery or table linen if something is missing.

During the night, your job is to keep the service from stepping on the key moments. No clearing the main course in the middle of the first dance, glasses filled before the toast, dessert not going out while everyone is dancing. That is discussed beforehand with whoever coordinates the event, because the party's timing is set by someone else and you translate it to the floor. How a wedding team is sized, from the point of view of whoever hires, is in [how many staff for a wedding](/en/blog/how-many-staff-for-a-wedding).

## What you coordinate at a gala dinner

At a gala dinner or a company dinner the clock is stricter. There are usually speeches, an awards ceremony or a show at a fixed time, and the service fits around them.

- **Service pauses.** While someone is speaking on stage, the floor doesn't move. You decide when to stop and when to resume.
- **Synchronised service.** Lots of waiters going in and out together: being in sync shows more than at a party.
- **Tables with protocol.** The table of officials or senior management is usually served first, and that has to be settled beforehand.
- **Less room to improvise.** If the main course has to be cleared before a speech, you work out the timing with the kitchen before you start.

## How to get there from waiter

Hardly anyone starts as a maître d'. You get there from the floor, and the path usually has similar steps:

1. **A bigger section.** You are given more tables or the top table because they know you don't fall behind.
2. **Section lead.** You are asked to guide new waiters or to cover a whole section.
3. **Second to the maître d'.** You work alongside the person running the room and learn how they divide it up and how they talk to the kitchen.
4. **Your own room.** First at small events, then at large ones.

What brings you closer: knowing every service format, getting on with the kitchen, being able to read a seating plan and staying calm at the peak. What holds you back: losing patience with the team in front of the guests.

Coordinating people has its own side (how to give short instructions, how to handle someone who didn't show up, how to earn the trust of whoever hires you). We cover that in [from event staff to team coordinator](/en/blog/from-event-staff-to-team-coordinator). The difference is that the maître d' does all of that inside the room, with table service at the centre.

## What to be clear on before accepting

Before confirming a date as maître d', ask about anything that isn't in the offer:

- **How many waiters you lead** and whether there are runners or bar support.
- **What time you arrive**: the maître d' usually arrives before the team to walk the room.
- **Who you talk to** on the organiser's side and on the kitchen side.
- **Whether you also coordinate other roles** (reception, cloakroom). If so, it has to be written down.
- **The pay** and how it is paid.

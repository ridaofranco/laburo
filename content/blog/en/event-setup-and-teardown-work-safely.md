Setup is the part of the event nobody sees: trucks unloaded in the early hours, stage platforms going up, miles of cable, and all of it again in reverse when the last light goes off. It's one of the most common ways into event work, and also the one that asks most of your body. This guide explains what the job demands and how to look after yourself, with what Argentine rules say. At the end there's a section for people who hire.

## What the job demands

**Hours that aren't everyone else's.** The build happens before the audience arrives, and often the space is only free the night before or from the early morning. Teardown starts when the audience leaves. That means shifts that start or end at odd hours and, at multi-day events, back-to-back days.

**Loads.** Equipment cases, platforms, panels, furniture, cable drums. A lot of what moves in a setup is lifted by hand, on the floor of a truck, on a ladder or in a narrow corridor.

**Delivery pressure.** The event has an opening time and it doesn't move. In the build the rush comes at the end; in teardown, at the start, because the space has to be handed back.

**Height.** Some tasks at height (hanging lighting, sound or structures) belong to specialised positions, like the rigger. If your position is setup or loading, don't climb up to do another position's job, even if you're asked in a hurry.

## Lifting weight: what Resolution 295/2003 says

**Resolution 295/2003** of Argentina's Ministry of Labour, Employment and Social Security has an annex on manual lifting of loads. What's important to understand:

- **There's no single maximum weight.** The limit values come from tables and depend on how many hours per day the task lasts, how many times per hour the load is lifted, the height the lift starts from and how far from the body the load is.
- **Lifting far away or high up is worse.** The rule says routine tasks shouldn't start more than 30 cm above the shoulder or more than 180 cm from the floor.
- **There are situations where the limit drops.** Among others: shifts of more than 8 hours, twisting the trunk while lifting, lifting with one hand, heat and humidity, unstable objects, poor grip and feet not properly planted. At a setup that's nearly all the time.
- **For some combinations there's no safe limit.** In certain cases of repeated lifting, the rule says outright that no safe limit is known.
- **If the values are exceeded, action is needed.** The resolution requires control measures when the limits are exceeded.

In day-to-day terms: **keep the load close to your body, don't twist with the weight on you, use trolleys and hand trucks when available, and anything heavy or unstable is lifted by two people.** Asking for help doesn't make you look bad: it's what the rule considers safe.

## Night work and rest

Argentina's **Employment Contract Law** has two rules worth knowing if you're employed:

- **Article 200:** a shift worked entirely at night cannot exceed seven hours, and night runs from 9 p.m. to 6 a.m.
- **Article 197:** there must be a break of at least twelve hours between the end of one shift and the start of the next.

Beyond the letter of the law, it's accumulated tiredness that injures people: when you're sleepy you misjudge a weight, step badly on the truck ramp and let go of a box too early. If you're offered teardown after working the build and the event, look carefully at how many hours you'll have behind you. For organising shifts and relief at long events there's [shift planning for long events](/en/blog/shift-planning-for-long-events).

## What's on you and what's on whoever hires

Argentina's **Law 19,587 on Health and Safety at Work** divides obligations:

- **The employer** has to take health and safety measures to protect workers' lives and integrity, keep machinery, installations and work tools in good condition, have means to give first aid immediately and train people on the risks of each task (articles 8 and 9).
- **The worker** has to follow safety rules and the instructions on protective equipment, and respect safety signs (article 10).

On equipment, Argentina's Occupational Risk Authority (SRT) says that **personal protective equipment must be provided by the employer**, and that the worker signs a receipt.

## How to make it to the end of teardown in one piece

- **Closed, sturdy footwear** and clothes that won't catch. If you're given protective equipment, use it.
- **Eat and drink water** even if nobody calls a break. Carry something with you.
- **Ask before moving** something you don't know: how much it weighs, where it goes, whether it comes apart.
- **Watch the floor**: cables, ramps, half-built platforms and poor light are the usual combination.
- **Speak up if something hurts** or you feel unwell. Carrying on is how a niggle becomes an injury.

If it's your first event, [first day working at an event](/en/blog/first-day-working-at-an-event) covers what to bring and how to present yourself.

## If you hire setup crews

- **Write down the real hours**, including teardown, and don't chain build, event and teardown on the same people without rest.
- **Size the crew by the loads**, not just by the time: heavy items take two.
- **Provide trolleys, hand trucks and ladders** in good condition, and define who does tasks at height.
- **Assign a setup lead** who decides the order and stops things when something isn't safe.

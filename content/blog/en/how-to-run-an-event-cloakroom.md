The cloakroom looks like the simplest position at an event until, at the end of the night, everyone wants their coat at the same time. This guide explains how to organise it, how to calculate how many people you need and what to do when something goes missing. At the end there's a section for people who do this job.

## How a cloakroom works

There's only one logic: **every item that comes in has a number, and the guest has that number in their hand.**

- **A two-part numbered ticket.** One half goes with the item (pinned, hung on the hanger or stuck to the bag) and the other half goes with the guest. Numbers have to be consecutive and never repeat during the event.
- **Numbered rails and hangers.** If each hanger has a fixed number and the rail is organised in blocks (1 to 100, 101 to 200), finding an item means going to an address, not searching.
- **Bags for what doesn't hang.** Backpacks, umbrellas, shopping bags, helmets. A bag with the same number stops small things getting mixed up.
- **Items you don't accept.** Cash, documents, phones, computers, jewellery, loose keys, medication. The organiser defines the list, it's written on a sign and the cloakroom applies it without exceptions. If a coat has something in its pockets, the guest is asked to take it out before handing it over.

## The arrival rush and, above all, the exit rush

On arrival, people come in waves and hanging is quick. On the way out, almost everyone leaves together, when the show ends or the lights come on, and handing back takes longer than taking in: read the number, go to the spot, unhook and come back. That's why **the cloakroom is sized for the exit, not the arrival.**

Two things flatten that peak:

1. **Open collection before the end.** If people are told they can collect before closing, the rush spreads out.
2. **Separate who fetches from who serves.** One person at the counter takes tickets and hands items back, and another goes to the rails. If everyone does everything, the counter is empty half the time.

## How to calculate how many points you need

There's no number of coats per person that works for every event. It depends on the setup (numbered hangers or not), the distance between the counter and the rails, the team's experience and how many guests leave something. What you can do is **measure the pace** and do the maths.

1. **Estimate how many people will leave something.** Winter and summer aren't the same; if you have records from a similar event, use them.
2. **Decide how many minutes you want the exit rush to clear in.**
3. **Time real hand-backs** at a trial run or a previous event, from the moment the guest gives the ticket until they leave with their item. Take the average.
4. **Divide:** people at the peak ÷ minutes = hand-backs per minute you need. That number ÷ the pace of one point = points that need to work at once.

**Example, with assumed numbers just to see the mechanics:** if 300 people leave a coat, you want the exit rush cleared in 30 minutes and you measure that one point hands back 2 items per minute, you need 10 hand-backs per minute, so 5 points working in parallel. Those 300, those 30 minutes and those 2 per minute are made up for the example: you measure your own.

On top of that, add one person to manage the queue and, at large events, a supervisor who handles complaints without holding up the counter. The full logic, with shifts and buffer, is in [how many staff for an event](/en/blog/how-many-staff-for-an-event).

## Signage

- **Where the cloakroom is**, visible from the entrance.
- **What isn't accepted**, in large print and before the counter.
- **From what time collection opens.**
- **"Have your ticket ready"** in the exit queue.
- If there are several counters, **which numbers each one handles.**

## Lost items and complaints

It's best to decide the procedure in advance:

- **Lost ticket:** handled separately, not in the main queue. Ask the guest to describe the item and what's inside it, and hand it back at the end, when few items are left and it's easy to identify. Who authorises that hand-back is decided by the organisers, not the cloakroom.
- **Wrong or missing item:** note the number, description and contact, and pass it to coordination.
- **What's left uncollected:** make a list with number and description, and hand it over to the organiser at closing. Nobody on the team takes anything.

## Common mistakes

- **Sizing for arrivals.** The team that's enough to hang coats isn't enough to hand them back.
- **Tickets that don't stay attached to the item, or rails with no numbered blocks.** Every hand-back becomes a search.
- **Not saying what isn't accepted.** The argument at the counter costs more than the sign.
- **Closing the cloakroom with the rest of the staff.** It has to stay open until the last guest leaves.

## If you work in a cloakroom

It's a job about order, memory and manner. What's valued most:

- **Being careful with numbers.** Tear, pin and hang the same way every time, even with a queue.
- **Calm at the exit rush.** A short greeting and a quick hand-back.
- **Friendly firmness** to say an item isn't accepted or that without a ticket the guest has to wait until the end.
- **Arriving early** to set up the counter and **staying until the end**: it's one of the last positions to close, and that should be in the hours you're offered.

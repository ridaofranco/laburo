A promo staff casting doesn't last long, but in those minutes whoever is hiring decides whether to put you in front of their brand. You don't need years of experience to get through it: you need to arrive prepared (the job is open to anyone), show how you talk to people and know what to ask before saying yes.

## What they assess

Every brand and every agency has its own way, but in general they look at this:

- **Presence.** It isn't about a body type: it's about arriving on time, neat, with good posture and enthusiasm. The first impression at the casting is the one the public will get at the activation.
- **Communication.** How you greet people, how you introduce yourself, whether you make eye contact, whether you know how to listen. At an activation you'll talk to hundreds of people who don't know you.
- **Handling the product.** They may give you a product or a brief and ask you to explain it in a few words. They're not looking for you to know everything: they want to see that you learn fast and get it across clearly.
- **Attitude to the unexpected.** A question you weren't expecting, someone who doesn't want to stop, a brief that changes. Staying calm is worth as much as the pitch.
- **Real availability.** They'll ask which days and times you can do. Answer with what you can actually commit to.

To understand what the brand asks of the agency before the casting, it helps to read [how to write the promo staff brief for a brand activation](/en/blog/brand-activation-brief-for-promo-staff): it covers the profile, the message and what gets measured.

## What to bring

The first thing is to read the whole call. The usual items are:

- **ID.**
- **An up-to-date CV or profile,** with your experience in customer service, sales, reception or languages.
- **Recent photos,** if they ask for them, without filters that change how you look.
- **Simple, neat clothes,** unless they give a dress code. If they say something specific (for example, black clothes or comfortable shoes), stick to it. More detail in [event staff dress code](/en/blog/event-staff-dress-code).
- **Something to take notes with,** because they may give you details about the brand or the hours.

If the call doesn't say what to bring, ask beforehand. Better that than turning up without something they asked for.

## How to prepare

1. **Research the brand.** Look at its website and social media: what it sells, who it talks to, what tone it uses. If they ask, you'll have something to say.
2. **Prepare a short introduction.** Name, area, what you've done and what you like about working with the public. Thirty seconds, without reading.
3. **Practise explaining a product.** Pick something you have at home and explain it out loud in a few sentences: what it is, what it's for, why try it. It's the most common exercise.
4. **Arrive early.** Being late to a casting is the worst possible sign for a job where punctuality is part of the role.
5. **Have your availability clear.** If you study or have another job, know in advance which dates you can do. There are ideas for organising yourself in [event jobs for students](/en/blog/event-jobs-for-students).

If you've never done this kind of work, also read [event jobs with no experience](/en/blog/event-jobs-with-no-experience).

## What to ask before accepting

Passing the casting isn't the same as accepting the job. Before confirming, be clear about:

- **How much it pays and per what unit:** day, hour or block.
- **When and how you get paid.** Approximate date and payment method.
- **Exact hours:** what time you have to arrive and what time it finishes, including setting up and closing the stand.
- **Where it is**, and how you get there and back.
- **Whether there's training beforehand,** how long it lasts and whether it's paid.
- **Who provides the uniform** and whether it has to be returned.
- **What exactly you have to do:** hand out samples, collect data, sell, give out material.

## Warning signs

Some things show that a call isn't serious or isn't worth it for you:

- **They ask you for money to work.** A paid compulsory course, a photo portfolio with a photographer they choose, a registration fee or a uniform you have to buy. Whoever hires you pays you: not the other way round.
- **They don't tell you how much they pay**, or say "we'll see later".
- **The hours have no end** or keep changing without explanation.
- **The casting is at a private place with no company details,** or they ask you to go alone to a private home.
- **They ask for data or photos that have nothing to do with the job.**
- **They rush you to decide** without giving you the basic information.

If something doesn't add up, don't accept. You can always say you'll think about it and reply later.

## After the casting

If you got it, confirm in writing, note down the hours and contact details, and go over [your first day working at an event](/en/blog/first-day-working-at-an-event). If you didn't, it isn't personal: very often they're looking for a very specific profile for a brand. What helps is having each experience well described on your profile, as [what to include in your event staff profile](/en/blog/event-staff-profile-what-to-include) explains.

A food truck solves an event's food without setting up a kitchen, brings movement to the space and people usually like it. But it brings things catering doesn't: a vehicle that has to get in, power, water, waste and a queue that forms by itself if you got the numbers wrong. This guide is for production companies, businesses and organisers thinking of adding one or several: when it makes sense, how many to book, what paperwork to ask for, what to agree with the venue and how to get quotes.

## When it makes sense and when it doesn't

It makes sense when:

- **The event is outdoors** or on a site with room to park the vehicles.
- **People move around**: a company party in a park, a fair, a festival, the after-party of a wedding.
- **You want variety** (several options at once) without building a kitchen.
- **The event is long** and people eat at different times.

Think twice when:

- **It's a served dinner**, with assigned tables and everyone eating at once. Catering with waiters usually works better there.
- **The venue has no way for a vehicle to get in** or the spot where it would park is far from the guests.
- **It rains and there's no cover** for the queue. If the event is outdoors, read the [rain plan for outdoor event staff](/en/blog/rain-plan-for-outdoor-event-staff).

## How many food trucks to book

As with the bar or valet parking, what matters isn't the total guest count but **how many eat at the same time**. You need three figures:

1. **How many people eat.** It isn't always the whole list: at an event with a cocktail reception first, some people arrive having eaten.
2. **The busiest time slot.** The hour when most orders come in. At a party it's usually right after opening; at a festival, between bands.
3. **How many dishes per hour each truck serves.** It depends on the menu: something made to order isn't the same as something ready to serve. The supplier has this figure; ask for it in writing when quoting.

**Example with assumed numbers** (just to show the calculation, not real data): if you estimate that 300 people eat in the first hour and each truck serves 100 dishes per hour with its menu, you need 3 trucks so nobody waits longer than is reasonable. If each one has a different offer, people won't split evenly: the longest queue will be at the most popular one, so the best-selling menu should be on two trucks or have more people inside.

The general logic of sizing by the peak is in [how many staff for an event](/en/blog/how-many-staff-for-an-event).

## What paperwork to ask for

Before confirming, ask by email for:

- **The municipal licence**, current and in the name of whoever invoices you. Each city has its own rules.
- **The food handler certificate** of each person working in the truck. The guide on the [food handler certificate for event work](/en/blog/food-handler-certificate-for-event-work) explains what it is; outside Argentina, the name and requirements depend on your country or city.
- **Fire extinguishers** with up-to-date charge and inspection, visible inside the vehicle.
- **Insurance**, with cover and validity.
- **Whether they invoice** and with what kind of receipt.

**In Buenos Aires City**, Law 5,707 regulates food vehicles:

- **Article 9:** the general licence authorises preparing and selling food and drink in the vehicle at private events and at public events organised privately.
- **Article 10:** it is issued by the Government Control Agency (AGC).
- **Article 13:** it lasts 1 year and has to be renewed.
- **Article 6:** everyone inside the vehicle must have passed the food handling course.
- **Article 11:** vehicle requirements include a drinking water tank of at least 50 litres, hot water, a waste water tank of at least 50 litres, electric cooking equipment, extinguishers certified under IRAM 3517 and at least two containers for sorting waste.

Selling on the street or in a square is a different matter: it requires a separate permit to use public space (article 14). If your event is in another city, ask for that municipality's licence and check with the local council what it requires for events.

## What to agree with the venue

A food truck is a vehicle: before you close anything, look at the venue with these questions.

- **Access.** Where it gets in, how high and wide the gate is, whether it has to cross grass or a ramp.
- **Ground.** Firm and level. On wet grass, a heavy vehicle sinks.
- **Power.** If it plugs into the venue's installation, how much power it needs and where the electrical panel is. If it brings its own generator, where it goes (away from people because of noise and fumes) and who supplies the fuel.
- **Water.** Whether it arrives with a full tank or needs a tap nearby.
- **Waste and liquids.** Where it throws rubbish, where it empties the waste water tank and who takes away the used oil. Nothing should be left for the next day.
- **Times.** When it gets in to set up, when it starts serving and when it leaves.

If you add several trucks, draw a simple plan with each one's position, each one's queue and where the bins go. Cleaning that area is a role in itself: it's covered in the guide on [cleaning staff for an event](/en/blog/cleaning-staff-for-an-event). For large events with many stalls, read [food festival staffing](/en/blog/food-festival-staffing).

## The arrangement: fixed price or selling to the public

There are two ways of working with a food truck, and they change everything:

- **Fixed price.** You pay, per person or for a number of dishes, and guests eat without paying. It's usual at weddings and company events. Agree how many dishes per person, what happens if there's food left over or it runs short, and how the truck tracks what it serves (tokens, wristbands, a list).
- **Selling to the public.** Guests pay for what they eat and the truck charges directly. It's usual at fairs and festivals. There's usually a fee or a percentage of sales for the organisers, and you should have in writing the prices it will charge and the payment methods it accepts.

Mixed formats also exist: a number of dishes paid for by the organisers and the rest on sale.

## What to ask when quoting

Put everything in a single request, with the event details (date, venue, hours, number of people, arrangement). Then ask:

1. **Menu and portions**, and whether there are gluten-free, vegetarian or vegan options.
2. **How many dishes per hour** it serves with that menu.
3. **What the price includes**: disposable tableware, napkins, drinks, staff, travel.
4. **What it needs from the venue**: power, water, space dimensions.
5. **Setup and pack-down times.**
6. **Paperwork**: licence, certificates, extinguishers and insurance.
7. **Deposit, payment terms and cancellation**, including cancellation for rain.

How to put that request together so you get fast, comparable replies is in [how to request quotes from event suppliers](/en/blog/how-to-request-quotes-from-event-suppliers).

## Common mistakes

- **Calculating by the total guest count** and not by the slot when people eat.
- **Not checking access** until the day of the event.
- **Not asking about power** and finding out the panel can't cope.
- **Leaving waste without an owner.**
- **Confirming without paperwork**, trusting that "they have it".

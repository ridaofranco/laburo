In Argentina, 16 and 17 year olds can work at events, but not at every event or at any hour. The law sets three limits that clash head-on with how events usually run: no night work between 8 p.m. and 6 a.m., working days of up to 6 hours with no overtime, and paperwork in order (parental consent, a school certificate and a medical certificate). In practice that rules out almost every party, wedding and concert, and leaves some daytime work: an afternoon brand activation, a trade fair, a morning sports event.

If you are an agency or brand from abroad bringing an event to Argentina, this matters because local labour law applies to the people hired for your event, wherever your company is based.

## The minimum age is 16

[Law 26,390](https://servicios.infoleg.gob.ar/infolegInternet/anexos/140000-144999/141792/norma.htm) raised the minimum age for employment to 16 and bans work by anyone under 16 in any form. There is no exception for "just one night". From 16 to 17 inclusive, it is classed as adolescent work and is allowed with restrictions.

The [Argentine government's FAQ on child and adolescent work](https://www.argentina.gob.ar/trabajo/orientacion/trabajo-infantil) says the same: adolescent work is allowed with special protections, and the only outright ban is on dangerous, arduous or unhealthy work.

## No night work

This is the point that weighs most at events. The law bans night work for under-18s, and the Employment Contract Law defines night as the period between 8 p.m. and 6 a.m. the following day. The government FAQ is direct: working hours must be in the afternoon and no night-time activity is allowed.

Picture a real event. An end-of-year party starting at 9 p.m. is entirely out. A wedding with a reception at 8 p.m. as well. A concert with doors at 7 p.m. leaves at most an hour of work before the limit, which helps nobody. What fits: a morning or afternoon event that finishes before 8 p.m., breakdown included.

## Six hours, no overtime

Under Law 26,390, a 16 or 17 year old's working day cannot exceed 6 hours a day or 36 a week. The government adds that overtime is not allowed and that extending the working day requires a request to the labour authority, which decides whether it is appropriate.

At events, what usually stretches is the end: the client asks for another hour, breakdown runs late, the return shuttle leaves later. With an adolescent on the team, none of that is possible. Give them a short shift with a fixed finish time, and make sure someone sees that they leave on time even if the rest of the crew stays.

## The paperwork

According to the government FAQ, an adolescent needs:

- **Consent from parents, guardians or carers.** Presumed if they live independently. The provincial or city labour authority can endorse it.
- **A school attendance certificate.** The employer must request it when hiring, and work cannot interfere with school.
- **A medical certificate** confirming they are fit for the task.
- **Formal registration**, like any other worker.

It is the employer who requests and keeps these documents, not the worker. For the rest of the paperwork for temporary staff, see [paperwork for hiring temporary event staff](/en/blog/paperwork-for-hiring-temporary-event-staff).

## Which roles make sense

Even in daytime, not every role works. Dangerous tasks are out: rigging at height, heavy lifting or equipment that can cause injury. A bar role makes no sense if alcohol is served.

What usually fits a daytime event: registration and reception, handing out material at an activation, support at a morning fair or conference, guiding the public at a sports event. Always with an adult in charge and the finish time written into the offer.

## If you are building the team

The simplest rule is to ask for date of birth when you call people and never assume. If someone is under 18, decide beforehand whether the event has a daytime slot that suits them; if not, say no clearly and explain why. And if an adolescent is already on the team, write down their finish time so nobody keeps them at the end.

The event is over, everything has been taken down and the production company is already thinking about the next date. That's exactly the moment when the most valuable information gets lost: who worked well, who arrived late, who solved a problem without anyone asking. This guide is for people who hire event staff and want every event to leave something written down for the next one.

## Why evaluate on the same day

A week later, out of a team of twenty you remember two: the one who shone and the one who didn't turn up. The rest end up as a "fine" that doesn't help you decide. If you write it down the same day, or the next morning, you still have the details: which post each person had, what time they arrived, what happened at the peak.

If the team is large, rely on whoever coordinated it on site. The coordinator saw things you didn't, and a ten-minute chat at the end is enough for them to pass on what matters. If you want to know what that role involves, we cover it in [from event staff to team coordinator](/en/blog/from-event-staff-to-team-coordinator), and when you need one in [when you need an event staff coordinator](/en/blog/when-you-need-an-event-staff-coordinator).

## What to look at: three things you can see

Evaluating isn't giving an opinion. It's recording what can be observed and what helps you decide whether to call someone again.

- **Punctuality.** Whether they arrived at the call time, not at doors open. Whether they gave notice in time when they had a problem. Whether they stayed until the end of the shift or left early.
- **Presence.** Whether they wore the clothing requested, were attentive to the public and at their post. Whether they looked available or were on their phone. In public-facing roles (reception, promo staff, bar) this weighs more.
- **Initiative.** Whether they sorted out small things without being asked: restocking, flagging that something was missing, helping a colleague at the peak, staying to help with teardown. It's what separates someone who just gets by from someone you can give more responsibility to.

Add a basic question to that: **did they follow the brief?** If the brief didn't say clearly what was expected, the failure may be yours and not theirs. In [what to check before hiring event staff](/en/blog/what-to-check-before-hiring-event-staff) we go over what's worth putting in writing beforehand.

## How to record it as soon as it ends

The rule that helps most: **facts, not adjectives.** "Arrived 20 minutes early and set up the registration desk alone" still makes sense in six months. "Very good" doesn't say for which post or why.

A simple guide for each person:

1. The post they covered and at which event.
2. One concrete fact about punctuality, presence or initiative (good or bad).
3. Would you call them again? For the same post or one with more responsibility?

You don't need to write a report. Two or three lines per person are enough, as long as you write them the same day.

## How to build your trusted list for the next date

With your notes up to date, the trusted list practically builds itself. A few ideas to make it really useful:

- **Organise by post, not just by person.** Someone excellent at reception may not perform the same at the bar. The favourite note is the place to make that clear.
- **Have replacements.** A trusted list with only one person per post isn't a list: it's a risk. When someone drops out at the last minute, what saves the event is having someone to call. We cover it in [what to do when event staff don't show up](/en/blog/event-staff-no-show-what-to-do).
- **Review it from time to time.** People's availability changes, they move or they stop working at events. Remove from your favourites anyone who no longer fits and add whoever worked well at the last event.
- **Call your list first.** For the next date, send offers to your favourites before opening the search to new people. That way the core of the team already knows how you work.

## How to give feedback

The private note is for you. The person who worked doesn't see it, so if you want them to improve, you have to tell them.

- **In private and soon.** A message or a call in the following days, not in front of the rest of the team.
- **With a concrete example.** "At the exit peak you left the cloakroom without telling anyone" is something that can be corrected. "You weren't committed enough" isn't.
- **The good things too.** Telling someone they did well and that you'll call them again is the cheapest way to keep them choosing to work with you.
- **With what you expect next time.** If something needs to change, make clear what and by when.

A booth at a trade show is a small event inside a big one. You have little space, many hours of open doors, a crowd walking past and a few seconds to get them to stop. The team you put inside decides whether those seconds turn into a conversation, a lead or nothing. This guide is for brands and agencies that exhibit: which roles you need, how to work out how many people and how to keep the team going when the show runs for several days.

## The roles at a booth

Not every booth needs every role, but it's worth thinking about them separately before combining them in one person.

- **Reception.** The person at the edge of the booth who greets visitors, asks what they're looking for and points them in the right direction. On LABURO you search for this as Hostess, Receptionist or Host.
- **Product demonstration.** Explains, shows or lets people try. Needs to know the product better than anyone else on the floor team. You search for this as Promo staff or Brand ambassador, and if there's product to taste, Sampling.
- **Lead capture.** Enters contacts in the form or tablet and explains what they'll be used for. At a small booth it can be the same person as reception, but at a booth with heavy foot traffic it's better for it to be its own post.
- **Booth coordination.** Builds the schedule, gives the first-day brief, covers absences, talks to the organiser and to your sales team. LABURO's catalogue has no role with that name: you search for Field producer or Production assistant, or pick someone from the team with previous trade show experience.
- **Setup and teardown.** Setup and teardown, Load-in and load-out. They work different hours from when the show is open, often the day before and the night it closes.

If the show draws visitors from abroad, add a Translator or interpreter, or ask for languages in the reception profile.

## How to work out the headcount

The most common mistake is thinking "we're a small booth, two people will do". The maths doesn't come from square metres: it comes from the hours and the service points.

1. **Count the points that have to be covered at the same time.** Counter, demo, lead capture. If the booth has two sides open to the aisle, you may need reception on both.
2. **Look at the show's real hours.** Not the public opening hours: the hours your team has to be inside, which start earlier and finish later.
3. **Cut those hours into shifts** according to what each role can sustain.
4. **Add the relief staff.** Every break leaves a point empty if nobody covers it. Relief staff are headcount, not an extra.
5. **Treat setup and teardown** as separate blocks, with their own people and their own hours.

The full calculation, step by step, is in [how many staff do you need for an event](/en/blog/how-many-staff-for-an-event).

## Rotation and breaks at multi-day shows

A three- or four-day show isn't one long day repeated. The first day brings nerves and adjustments, the middle ones may be calmer and the last usually brings more visitors with a more tired team.

- **Build the schedule by position, not by person.** First the points and their time slots, then the names.
- **Overlap the reliefs.** The incoming person arrives a few minutes before the one going on break leaves, so the booth is never empty just as a visitor approaches.
- **Write the hours in the offer**, with call time and estimated finishing time, for each day.
- **Keep the same people across days** when you can. Someone who already knows the product and the booth performs better on day two than someone new.
- **Give a short brief every morning:** what changed, what questions came up, what was missing.

All of this is developed in [shift planning for long events](/en/blog/shift-planning-for-long-events).

## Lead capture without problems

If the booth collects names, emails or phone numbers, the team needs to know what to say before entering them. In Argentina, Law 25,326 on the Protection of Personal Data requires consent to be free, express and informed, and requires that, before the data is collected, the person is told, among other things, what it will be used for, who may receive it, who is responsible for the database and that they can request access, correction or deletion.

In practice: write that text into the brief, keep it visible on the tablet or form, and make sure nobody on the team improvises promises to get one more contact. How to put that brief together is in [how to write a brand activation brief for promo staff](/en/blog/brand-activation-brief-for-promo-staff).

## What to ask the organiser

Every trade show has its own exhibitor rules, and it's not safe to assume they're the same as the last one's. Before confirming the team, ask in writing:

- **How exhibitor staff are accredited** and the deadline for submitting names.
- **What details they need** for each person.
- **Whether there's a badge quota per booth** and what happens if you need to add someone at the last minute.
- **Access times** for setup, for the team before opening and for teardown.
- **Where staff come in** and whether there are rules on dress, bringing in stock or using sound at the booth.

## Common mistakes

- **Sizing by square metres instead of hours.** Two people can run a small booth, but not all day without relief.
- **Combining demo and lead capture in one person at a busy booth.** While they're explaining, the queue of people who only wanted to leave their details walks away.
- **Not accrediting the team in time.** A name that wasn't submitted in time may be left at the door.
- **Forgetting teardown.** On closing night everyone wants to leave, and the booth gets taken down with fewer people than it needs.
- **Sending people who don't know the product.** A brief at the door is no substitute for prior training.

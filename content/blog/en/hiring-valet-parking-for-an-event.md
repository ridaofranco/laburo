Valet parking is the first thing a guest sees on arrival and the last thing when they leave. If it works, nobody notices. If it fails, the line of cars in the street, the key that can't be found or the car with a scratch are what the guest takes home from the event. This guide is for production companies, businesses and organisers adding valet parking to a wedding, a gala, a launch or a corporate event: what it does, how many people you need, how to look after the keys and what paperwork to ask for.

## What the valet does

Four tasks, in this order:

- **Receiving.** The guest pulls up at the door, the valet opens it, greets them, looks over the car and hands over the ticket.
- **Parking.** Takes the car to the agreed spot (the venue's car park, a nearby car park, a reserved area) and comes back to the door.
- **Safekeeping.** Keys are logged and kept in one place, under the care of one person.
- **Returning.** At the exit, the guest hands over the ticket, the valet fetches the car and delivers it at the door.

Around that: managing traffic at the entrance so cars don't pile up in the street, helping with bags or packages, and telling coordination if the parking area fills up sooner than expected.

At LABURO the role is called **valet parking**. If the event transports guests (from a hotel, from a distant car park), add **shuttle driver** or **driver**. The transport roles appear alongside all the others in [event staff roles and positions](/en/blog/event-staff-roles-and-positions).

## How many people you need

The total number of cars isn't enough: what matters is **how many arrive together**. At a wedding or a gala, almost everyone arrives within the same half hour and leaves within the same half hour. You need three figures:

1. **How many cars you expect.** It comes from the guest list and from how many are coming in their own car (ask in the RSVP).
2. **The busiest arrival window.** What share of those cars arrives in the first hour, and the same for departure.
3. **The time for one full round trip.** Receiving, driving to the spot, parking, leaving the key and walking back to the door. It depends on the distance: a car park inside the venue is not the same as one two blocks away.

**An example with assumed figures** (only to show the calculation, not real data): you expect 80 cars and estimate that 56 arrive in the first hour. If one full round trip takes 8 minutes, one valet moves about 7 cars an hour. For 56 cars in that hour you need 8 valets driving, plus one person at the key desk who doesn't drive. If almost everyone leaves together, the team has to be at full strength again at that moment, not running at half capacity.

The hours in between are usually quiet. That is when breaks are organised, but nobody leaves before the end: the departure peak is the one that shows most. How to build that grid is in [shift planning for long events](/en/blog/shift-planning-for-long-events), and the general logic in [how many staff for an event](/en/blog/how-many-staff-for-an-event).

## The key log

This is where the guest's trust is most at stake. A simple system that works:

| Step | What's done |
|---|---|
| Numbered ticket | One half for the guest, the other tied to the key |
| Log sheet | Number, number plate, time of arrival, where the car was left |
| Car's condition | Visible scratches or dents noted on receipt, with the guest present if possible |
| Board or box | Keys ordered by number, in a locked or supervised place |
| One person only | Only whoever is at the key desk hands them out |
| Departure | The number is crossed off the log sheet when the car is returned |

Two more rules: the car is only handed over against the ticket, and if someone has lost it, it is sorted out with the person at the key desk and coordination, never on the pavement in a hurry.

## The driving licence

Whoever drives guests' cars has to be licensed. What Argentina's National Traffic Law 24,449 says:

- **Article 16:** the **class B** licence is the one for cars and vans. If the event takes larger vehicles, check which class the law requires for that type.
- **Article 11:** for class B you must be at least 17.
- **Article 40, point a:** to drive, the driver must be licensed for that type of vehicle and carry the licence.
- **Article 48, point a:** bans driving any vehicle with a blood alcohol level above zero (text as amended by Law 27,714). For the valet team that means zero alcohol throughout the shift, no exceptions.
- **Article 48, point b:** bans letting unlicensed people drive. If someone on the team doesn't have a valid licence, they don't drive.

In practice: ask for a valid licence when you book people, check the class and expiry date, and make sure each valet carries it on the day. Outside Argentina, the licence rules depend on local law.

## What to agree with the venue

- **Where cars are parked** and how many spaces there really are.
- **Where cars enter and leave**, and whether anything needs closing off or signposting.
- **Where the key desk goes**, with cover and light.
- **What happens if it fills up**: a second spot, checked out in advance.

If the event is in a hotel, the hotel may have its own service: clarify it beforehand, as explained in [staffing a hotel event](/en/blog/staffing-a-hotel-event).

## Common mistakes

- **Calculating by the total number of cars** rather than by the arrival window.
- **A half-strength team at departure**, when everyone leaves together.
- **Keys without numbers**, or handed out by anyone on the team.
- **Not noting the car's condition** on receipt.
- **Not asking for the licence** until the day of the event.
- **A parking area nobody visited**, which turns out further away or smaller than it seemed.

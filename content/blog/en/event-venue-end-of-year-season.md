November and December are the months when requests pile up most for a venue: company end-of-year parties, graduation parties and celebrations for groups who want to close the year together. The difference between an orderly season and a season of problems lies in what you do beforehand: how you build the calendar, how you handle the hours and what you put in writing. This guide doesn't give demand figures (every venue knows its own); it gives a method.

In Argentina, where LABURO operates, this is the busiest stretch of the year for events, right before summer.

## The three types of event in the season

Not all end-of-year requests are the same, and it's worth knowing which ones your venue suits before they arrive.

- **Company end-of-year parties.** Usually on weekdays or Fridays, starting after office hours or at night. The person enquiring is often someone from HR or a production company, and they need a clear proposal to get it approved internally. On the staffing side, what they need is covered in [staffing a company end-of-year party](/en/blog/staffing-a-company-end-of-year-party).
- **Graduation parties.** A young crowd, often with families involved in the organisation, and sometimes with minors. Before accepting, check what your permit allows and what access control and closing time you'll require.
- **Year-end events for groups, clubs or families.** Smaller and more varied. These are the easiest to move to another date if the one they asked for is taken.

If your venue doesn't suit one of these, say so in your listing. It saves you enquiries you won't be able to take.

## How to organise dates and hours

In peak season, the calendar is your most valuable asset. A few rules that keep things in order:

1. **One calendar, one person who confirms.** If two people at the venue can confirm a date, sooner or later they'll confirm the same one.
2. **Every booking with four times:** arrival for setup, event start, event end and supplier departure. Clients usually only think about the second and third; the venue lives in the first and the fourth.
3. **"On hold" dates with an expiry.** If you hold a date without a deposit, set the day it's released and say so in writing. A date held with no deadline is a date you can't offer.
4. **The most requested dates, with clear terms.** If some December days have different booking terms, they should be written from the first reply and not appear later.

## Double slots: when yes and when no

Hosting two events on the same day (one at lunchtime and another at night, for example) can make sense in peak season. It only works if the changeover is planned:

- **The first event has an end time and a supplier departure time**, and both are in the agreement.
- **Between the two there's time to clear, clean and set up again**, with the people who'll do it already confirmed. The exact time depends on your venue and how much has to be taken down: measure it on a real changeover, don't assume it.
- **The second client knows what time they can come in to set up**, and isn't promised an earlier time.
- **Overtime for the first event has a cap**, because it can't run over without eating into the second.

If the second event's setup is long (stage, technical gear, heavy decoration), the double slot probably won't fit. Better to say no to one slot than to have two angry clients on the same day. To plan the team's shifts when the day is long, see [shift planning for long events](/en/blog/shift-planning-for-long-events).

## What to ask for and put in writing

In December everything speeds up, and what was agreed over the phone is the first thing forgotten. Before confirming a date, put this in writing with the client:

- **The date and the four times** (setup, start, end, departure).
- **Number of people and layout**: seated, standing, with a dance floor.
- **What the hire includes** and what the client brings or hires.
- **Which suppliers will come in**, at what time and through which entrance.
- **The deposit, when the balance is paid** and what happens if the date is cancelled or changed.
- **What happens if the event runs over**, and how far it can run over.
- **Who the client's contact is on the day**, with a phone number.

You don't need a long contract: it's enough for these points to be in an email the client replies to saying they agree. For the tone and order of that reply, see [how to answer a venue inquiry](/en/blog/how-to-answer-a-venue-inquiry).

## Your listing up to date before the season starts

People looking for a venue for the end of the year usually contact several at once and decide quickly. Your listing has to answer the basics before they write to you:

- **Recent photos** of the venue set up for the type of event you want to host. If you've changed something (furniture, paint, lighting), old photos work against you.
- **Capacity by layout**, honestly. If they ask for 200 and you fit 150 seated, better that they know beforehand.
- **What's included** and which types of event it suits.
- **The description**, reviewed: it shouldn't promise something you won't be able to deliver in December.

A product launch is short and has a lot of people watching: press, the brand's guests, sometimes content creators streaming live. There's no second take. That's why launch staff are put together differently from a party's: fewer people serving and more people who need to know exactly what to say, where to stand and who to refer people to. This guide covers each role, the brief and the rehearsal.

## Press and guest registration

Registration is the first contact and sets how the event begins.

- **Two lists, two desks if possible.** Press on one side and guests on the other. Press tend to arrive with equipment, ask for materials and want to get set up quickly; guests, on the other hand, want to say hello and go in.
- **What each one receives.** Badge, press kit, product kit. Registration should have it all sorted by list before opening.
- **Anyone not on the list.** Referred to a person from the brand; the desk doesn't decide.

[What conference registration staff do](/en/blog/conference-registration-staff-role) explains how to measure the desk's pace at the arrival peak, and the logic is the same.

## Hostesses

Hostesses welcome, direct and escort: they take press to their spot, guide guests to the product area and announce when the presentation starts. They don't present the product; that's what promo staff are for.

[Corporate event hostess and reception](/en/blog/corporate-event-hostess-and-reception) explains the difference between a hostess, promo staff and registration staff, which helps you avoid asking for one role and expecting another.

## Promo staff trained on the product

At a launch, promo staff don't hand out flyers: they show the product to people who don't know it yet, sometimes to journalists. They'll be asked how it works, how it's different, how much it costs and when it reaches the shops.

For that they need:

1. **Material from the brand** with what they can say, what they can't (unconfirmed prices, dates, comparisons with competitors) and answers to the most likely questions.
2. **Time with the product in hand** before the doors open. Reading a fact sheet isn't enough.
3. **A rule for what they don't know:** "the brand team can answer that for you", and refer.

How to write all that is in [how to write a brand activation brief for promo staff](/en/blog/brand-activation-brief-for-promo-staff).

## Runners

Runners move whatever needs moving: restocking kits at registration, taking the product to the stage, finding a speaker, walking a lost guest. At a launch with a tight schedule, they're what stops someone from coordination having to leave their post. Everything about the role is in [what does an event runner do](/en/blog/what-does-an-event-runner-do).

## Technical crew

Sound, lighting and video (screens or LED). At a launch, tech isn't decoration: the product presentation usually depends on a video, a lighting change or a microphone that has to work first time.

Ask for the technical team to be at the full rehearsal, not just the setup, and for one person to call the changes during the presentation.

## Who talks to the press

This rule is decided beforehand and written down:

- **The brand designates who speaks:** a spokesperson, the PR team or whoever is appropriate.
- **Hired staff don't give statements** or share opinions about the product with a journalist, even if they know it well. If asked, they refer.
- **All staff know who that person is** and how to find them during the event. A runner can be the one who goes to get them.

It seems obvious, but an enthusiastic promo staff member who answers a question about the price can end up in a news story with a figure the brand hadn't confirmed.

## The brief

A single document for all staff, with a common section and a section per role:

- A minute-by-minute schedule, with the key moments marked (opening, presentation, end of press time).
- A floor plan showing where each role stands.
- Who coordinates and who each person reports to.
- The press rule.
- Dress code: [event staff dress code](/en/blog/event-staff-dress-code) has ideas for setting it.
- Arrival, rehearsal, event and teardown times.

## The rehearsal

The rehearsal is when the brief becomes real. It should be at the venue, with the tech working and the whole staff present:

1. Walk the venue, everyone at their position.
2. Run through the full schedule, with the lighting and video changes.
3. Promo staff practise with the product.
4. A short dry run of press arrivals: who receives, who escorts, who refers.

The rehearsal is work and has to be included in the hours and pay in the offer. If it's on a different day, say so from the start.

## Common mistakes

- **Hiring promo staff the day before** and expecting them to know the product.
- **Not deciding who talks to the press.**
- **Rehearsing without the tech** or without the full staff.
- **A different brief for each role** with no common section: everyone knows their part and nobody knows the schedule.

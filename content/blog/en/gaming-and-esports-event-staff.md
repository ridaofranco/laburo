A gaming or esports event is like a trade fair, a concert and a TV studio all at once. There's a crowd that arrives in waves, gaming stations that can't fail, a live broadcast and, often, kids and teenagers with their families. This guide is for production companies, agencies and brands that have to build that team: which posts to cover, how to think about access with a young crowd and how to sustain long days.

## The positions you can't do without

**Registration.** Players, teams, press, content creators, sponsors and the general public come in through different routes. **Registration staff** check lists, hand out badges and sort out the cases that aren't on them. If tickets use QR codes, add a **Scanner or ticketing operator**, and if wristbands are handed out by area, **Wristbands and badges**. What the job involves is covered in [what conference registration staff do](/en/blog/conference-registration-staff-role): the logic is the same.

**Access control.** General entrance, players' entrance, stage area, backstage. Each with a clear rule about who gets through.

**Gaming stations.** Free play or demo areas need people to keep the queue in order, explain the game in two sentences, time each turn and flag when a setup freezes. **Host**, **Promo staff** or **Brand ambassador** work for this post, the last one if the brand wants someone who knows the product.

**Technical crew.** Sound, screens and broadcast are the heart of the event. The roles are **Sound technician**, **Video or LED technician**, **Switcher operator** and **Streaming or broadcast**. If there are casters or commentators, add **Announcer or booth**. This team is put together well in advance and confirmed with a technical rehearsal the day before.

**Runners.** They bring a spare controller, a cable, water to the booth, a printed list to the door. At an event with many stations, without runners everything stalls. What they do and how they're organised is in [what does an event runner do](/en/blog/what-does-an-event-runner-do).

**Stage and audience.** An **Entertainer** or **Host** to introduce matches and fill the dead time, and a **Live community manager** if the brand wants social media covered during the event.

**What tends to be forgotten.** **Cloakroom** (the crowd arrives with backpacks and jackets), **Merchandise sales**, **Cleaning staff** for the gaming areas, and a **First aider** or **Nurse** depending on the size and what the venue requires.

## A young crowd and minors: the rule goes in writing

Many gaming events have attendees under 18. That doesn't complicate the event, but it means deciding beforehand what at another event gets sorted out at the door:

- **Age by area.** Which zones are for all ages and which aren't. If a tournament or area has a minimum age, it should be at the entrance and on the sign.
- **Minors alone or accompanied.** If a responsible adult is required, how they're identified (matching wristband colour, linked badge) and what happens if they get separated.
- **Meeting point.** A fixed spot the whole team knows, for kids who get lost. Who stays with the child until the adult turns up, also in writing.
- **A bar serving alcohol.** In Argentina, Law 24,788 bans the sale of alcoholic drinks to under-18s nationwide (article 1). If there's a bar, the badge has to show who can buy and the bar team has to be able to refuse without arguing.
- **Image.** Argentina's Law 26,061 recognises the right of children and adolescents to respect for their own image and bans publishing images that identify them against their will and their parents' when it harms their dignity or reputation (article 22). At an event with a live stream, say at registration that there will be cameras and make sure the streaming team knows what to do if a family asks not to appear.

The door team doesn't make these rules: it applies them. That's why they have to be in the brief, the same for every shift. If the event is specifically for children, also read [staffing a kids' event](/en/blog/staffing-a-kids-event).

## How many people to put on

You work it out by pace, not by gut feeling. Three figures drive it:

1. **How many people arrive in the opening hour** and during the finals. That's when the queue forms.
2. **How many gaming stations are open at once.** Each demo area with a queue needs at least one person keeping order.
3. **How many hours the day lasts**, to plan reliefs without closing posts.

**Example with assumed numbers** (just to show the maths, not real data): if you expect 600 people in the first hour and one registration agent with a scanner handles about 150 an hour, you need 4 at that moment; after opening you can drop to 2 and send the others to the gaming stations. With 6 demo areas, 6 people plus 2 for relief. Add tech, runners and coordination, and measure on day one to adjust day two. The general logic is in [how many staff do you need for an event](/en/blog/how-many-staff-for-an-event).

## Long shifts and finals that run over

A tournament doesn't end at the time on the schedule: matches go long, there are technical pauses and finals get pushed back. Three things help:

- **Shifts with reliefs** at the posts that can't be left empty: door, gaming stations, tech booth.
- **A finishing time written** into the offer, and what happens if the event runs over.
- **Real breaks**, with food and water, especially for the tech crew, who can't leave the booth just anytime.

How to build the schedule is in [shift planning for long events](/en/blog/shift-planning-for-long-events). If the event is inside an exhibition centre, [trade show booth staff](/en/blog/trade-show-booth-staff) is useful too.

## Common mistakes

- **One entrance for everyone.** Players and press get stuck in the public queue.
- **An age rule that's spoken, not written.** Each shift applies it differently.
- **No meeting point** for lost kids.
- **Tech confirmed at the last minute**, with no prior rehearsal.
- **Gaming stations with no time control.** The queue doesn't move and people leave.
- **No runners.** A broken cable holds up a match for twenty minutes.

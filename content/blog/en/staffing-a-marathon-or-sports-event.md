A marathon, a road race or a tournament has something a party doesn't: the event happens in several places at once and each place has its own pressure point. Kit pickup peaks the day before, bag drop an hour before the start, the finish when the bulk of the runners come in. This guide sorts out the posts, how to calculate them and what to decide before calling people in.

## The team's blocks

Think of it as stations, each with its own lead:

- **Kit pickup.** Usually happens before race day. Find the number, check identity, hand over the bib, timing chip if there is one, T-shirt and bag. It's a counter with a queue: you calculate it like any service post.
- **Participant registration.** Checking who's signed up, category or size changes, the cases that don't appear on the list. It's the post that solves problems, not the one that hands things out. To see how registration work is done, read [what conference registration staff do](/en/blog/conference-registration-staff-role).
- **Water stations.** Setting up tables, cups or bottles ready before the first runner passes, constant restocking and clearing the stretch once the last one has gone by.
- **Start.** Getting runners into their pens or waves, making sure nobody sneaks in from the side and clearing the area in seconds when the race starts.
- **Finish.** The busiest point: medals, final water, making sure nobody stops on the line and the exit funnel doesn't jam.
- **Signage and course marshalling.** People at crossings, bends and diversions marking the route and reporting by radio. A crossing with nobody on it is the most noticeable mistake.
- **Bag drop.** Backpacks and coats, organised by bib number. We go into it below.
- **Coordination.** One person per block who knows the map, has a radio and makes decisions without asking about everything.

LABURO's catalogue has roles that fit several of these blocks: Registration staff, Access control, Cloakroom, Wristbands and badges, Runner, Volunteer coordinator, Cleaning staff and, under health, First aider, Nurse and Doctor. The medical team and what each authority requires are decided by the organisers with whoever is responsible: this guide doesn't cover that.

## Calculate by measured pace, not by eye

The question isn't "how many participants do I have" but **how many reach each post at its worst moment and how many one person can serve per minute.** It's the same method as in [how many staff do you need for an event](/en/blog/how-many-staff-for-an-event), applied station by station.

1. **Measure one post's pace.** Time real handovers: how long it takes one person to find a kit and hand it over, or to take a backpack and give out the ticket. Do it in a trial with your set-up (tables, boxes, number order) or take it from a previous edition.
2. **Estimate the peak.** How many participants you expect in each station's busiest hour. If you have data from past editions, use it; if not, start from the times you announce (kit pickup window, start time).
3. **Divide.** People per minute at the peak, divided by those one post serves per minute: that gives how many posts must be open at once for the queue not to grow.
4. **Add restocking and relief.** A post that serves people needs someone to bring it material, and long shifts need reliefs. How to set them up is in [shift planning for long events](/en/blog/shift-planning-for-long-events).

On the course the calculation is different: it's by points, not by pace. You count crossings, diversions and water stations on the map and assign people to each, plus someone mobile to cover gaps.

## The race bag drop

It looks like any event cloakroom, with two differences. First, people leave backpacks, so you need bags or organised floor space, not just rails. Second, the number already exists: **the bib.** Organising by bib ranges turns each collection into going to an address.

The peak is at the end, when tired, cold runners arrive. If the bag drop was sized for the morning drop-off, on the way back you get the longest queue of the day. The details of the set-up are in [how to run an event cloakroom](/en/blog/how-to-run-an-event-cloakroom).

## Paid staff and volunteers

Many races and tournaments combine paid staff with volunteers. Which arrangement fits each task and what obligations you have towards each group is a question for your legal or employment adviser: we don't settle it here.

What is operational and applies in any case:

- **Critical posts with experienced people and confirmed hours.** Registration, finish and block coordination aren't left to first-timers.
- **A volunteer coordinator** who welcomes, assigns and briefs, separate from overall coordination.
- **A written brief for everyone**, with the map, each station's hours and who to call on the radio.
- **A replacement list.** If someone doesn't turn up, [event staff no-show: what to do](/en/blog/event-staff-no-show-what-to-do) explains the steps.

## Common mistakes

- **Sizing kit pickup by total entries** instead of by how many come in the busiest window.
- **One coordinator for the whole course.** If they go off to a problem at the start, nobody decides at the finish.
- **Water stations set up late.** The first runner comes through long before the bulk.
- **Dismantling the finish before the last runner comes in.** It's the image the person who worked hardest goes home with.

If the event is outdoors and long, much of it resembles [outdoor festival staffing](/en/blog/outdoor-festival-staffing): shade, water and reliefs for your own team.

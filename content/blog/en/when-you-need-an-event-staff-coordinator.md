There comes a point when the event no longer fits in your head. You're with the client, the stage is calling you, a bar has run out of ice and someone on the door asks if they can go and eat. If all of that goes through you, the team is left with nobody in charge just when it needs someone most. That's what a staff coordinator is for. This guide explains what they do, the signs your event needs one and how to set up the chain of command so it works.

## What a staff coordinator does

The coordinator doesn't work a position: they make sure **every** position is covered and working.

- **They confirm the call-out.** They know who's coming, at what time and to which position, and check it again the day before.
- **They give the briefing.** Before opening, they gather the team and explain what the event is, what each person does, who to ask and what not to do.
- **They build the shift and break rota.** Who's at each position, when they go to eat and who covers them.
- **They check who has arrived** and who is at their post throughout the event.
- **They handle dropouts.** They rearrange the rota and tell production if a replacement is needed.
- **They filter questions.** The team asks them, not you. They solve what they can and pass up what they can't.
- **They close the day.** Equipment and badges returned, nobody left without saying, and a short report for production.

If you want the role in detail from the point of view of whoever does it, it's in [from event staff to team coordinator](/en/blog/from-event-staff-to-team-coordinator).

## Signs your event needs one

There's no headcount above which it becomes compulsory. What there are, are clear signs:

1. **You'll be busy with something else.** If your place during the event is with the client, on stage or at the tech desk, nobody is watching the team.
2. **There are several separate areas.** Door, bar, main room, cloakroom, backstage. If you can't see them all from one spot, someone has to walk round them.
3. **There are overlapping shifts.** Setup, event and teardown with different people, or relief staff in the middle of the night. Every shift change is a moment when positions get lost.
4. **The team is new.** People who haven't worked together or with you need a clear reference for who to ask.
5. **The event is long.** More hours mean breaks, meals and tiredness that someone has to manage. The logic is in [shift planning for long events](/en/blog/shift-planning-for-long-events).
6. **This happened to you at your last event:** empty positions without anyone noticing, everyone going to eat at the same time, questions reaching you ten at a time.

If you recognise two or three of these, the coordinator isn't an extra cost: it's what stops you paying a team that's only half working.

## How to set up the chain of command

The chain has to be short and known to everyone. A structure that works for most events:

- **Production** (you or your producer): decides about the event, talks to the client and the suppliers.
- **Staff coordination:** one person in charge of the whole team. They're the only one who talks to production about staff matters.
- **Area leads,** when the event is large or has separate areas: someone from the team who's put in charge of a bar, a door or a group, and reports to the coordinator.
- **The rest of the team:** each person knows who their area lead is or, if there isn't one, who the coordinator is.

Three rules to make the chain work:

1. **Each person has only one boss during the event.** If production gives orders directly to the bar while the coordinator says something else, the team doesn't know who to listen to.
2. **Everyone knows who's who.** It's said at the briefing and repeated: "any questions, go to her; if you can't find her, go to him".
3. **The coordinator must be able to reach each area lead within a few minutes.** If they can't, you need more area leads, not faster running.

## What you need to give the coordinator

A coordinator without information is just one more member of staff. Before the event, give them:

- **The headcount by role and by time slot**, with names and contacts.
- **The venue plan** with the areas, storage, toilets and exits.
- **The event schedule**, including the peak moments.
- **The brief:** what the event is, dress code, what can and can't be done. For activations, the template is in [how to write the promo staff brief for a brand activation](/en/blog/brand-activation-brief-for-promo-staff).
- **Which decisions they can take alone** (moving people between posts, covering a break) and which they have to check with you (adding someone, letting someone leave early).
- **An earlier call time than everyone else**, because they arrive first and leave last.

## Common mistakes

- **Making whoever is best at the bar the coordinator.** Being good at one position isn't the same as watching the team. If you take them off the bar, you've lost your best bartender.
- **A coordinator who ends up working a position.** Helping for a while is fine; staying there isn't.
- **Going around the coordinator.** If you give instructions directly to the team, you undermine their authority in front of everyone.
- **Not asking for the closing report.** It's the best information you'll have for the next event and for [evaluating your staff after the event](/en/blog/how-to-evaluate-event-staff).

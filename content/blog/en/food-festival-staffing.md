A food festival has two teams working at the same time: the one at each stall, which cooks and sells, and the organiser's team, which keeps the site running. This guide is about the second one. If you are putting together a market with stalls and food trucks, here are the roles you almost always need, what each one does and what to put in writing before you open.

## First, separate what each stall provides from what the organiser provides

The most common mistake is assuming. Before you call anyone, write this into the exhibitor rules:

- **Who runs the stall** (always the exhibitor) and whether their people need a credential.
- **Who cleans what.** The usual arrangement is that each stall keeps its own space and the organiser looks after shared areas, bins and the waste area. If it isn't written down, the seating area ends up belonging to nobody.
- **Who takes payment.** Whether each stall charges on its own or there is a central token or card system.
- **Load-in and load-out times**, and where vehicles park.

With that clear, you know which roles are yours.

## Stall and exhibitor check-in

It is the first shift of the day and the one that organises everything else. Whoever handles check-in:

- Receives each stall or food truck against a closed list (name, person in charge, assigned pitch, number of people).
- Hands credentials or wristbands to the stall's staff, so access control later knows who is an exhibitor and who is a visitor.
- Points out the pitch and the load-in deadline, and tells coordination when someone hasn't arrived.

At a festival that runs over several days, it helps if the same person does check-in every day: they already know the exhibitors and sort things out quickly. If you want the details of the job, see [what conference registration staff do](/en/blog/conference-registration-staff-role), which works the same way with exhibitors.

## Access control, if there is an entrance

If the festival is free and open, information points at the entrances may be enough. If there is a paid ticket, a capacity limit or wristbands, you need access control: scanning or tearing tickets, counting people if the site has a maximum, and someone who keeps the exhibitor entrance separate from the public one.

## Cleaning and waste

At a food event, cleaning is not a detail: it is what people see on the tables. What has to be covered:

- **Tables and shared areas** throughout the day, not only at closing.
- **Bins**: empty them before they overflow. At the lunch or evening peak they fill much faster.
- **The stalls' waste area** (oil, boxes, scraps), kept away from the public.
- **Toilets**, if they are inside the site.

How many people you need depends on the size of the site and the flow, and you adjust it by watching the first day. The logic of shifts, supplies and what to ask for is in [cleaning staff for an event](/en/blog/cleaning-staff-for-an-event).

## Restocking runners

Runners are the ones who move things: ice, bin bags, cleaning supplies, water for staff and, if the organiser offers it, stock from a shared storeroom to the stalls. Without runners, every stall leaves its post at the worst moment to go and get something. Give them a set route and a direct line to coordination. More on the role in [what an event runner does](/en/blog/what-does-an-event-runner-do).

## Cash desks or tokens, if there is a central system

If the festival sells tokens, cards or credits that are then spent at the stalls, you need cashiers at fixed points. What to decide beforehand:

- **How many desks and where**, away from the stall queues so they don't cross.
- **How the cash is counted and handed over** at the end of each shift, and who receives it.
- **What happens to unused tokens**, written on a visible sign.

If each stall charges on its own, this role isn't yours to cover.

## Visitor information

One or two people who know where everything is (toilets, cash desks, gluten-free or vegetarian stalls if there are any, the exit, first aid) take a load off everyone else. It is a good spot for someone who is easy to talk to and knows the site map by heart.

## Food handler certificate for anyone touching food

In Argentina, according to ANMAT, anyone who is or may be in contact with food must hold a food handler certificate. At a festival that covers whoever cooks, serves or plates up at the stalls, and also whoever hands out food or drink tastings if the organiser offers them. The Argentine certificate is valid nationwide for three years from the date it is issued. The course lasts at least 7 hours and the assessment is always in person.

If your festival has its own tasting area, that team has its own rules: see [how to build a sampling team](/en/blog/how-to-build-a-sampling-team). The step by step for getting the certificate is in [food handler certificate for event work](/en/blog/food-handler-certificate-for-event-work).

## If the festival is outdoors

Heat, shade for fixed stalls, water for staff and a rain plan decided in advance: all of that applies just as it does at a festival. It is covered in [outdoor festival staffing](/en/blog/outdoor-festival-staffing).

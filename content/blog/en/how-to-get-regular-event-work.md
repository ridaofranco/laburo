Doing odd jobs means taking whatever comes up. Getting called regularly is something else: it means that when a producer has to fill a position, they think of you before opening the search.

This isn't about charisma. It's about what happens on the other side of the screen, and about five or six very concrete habits.

## What happens on the other side

When a producer opens the pool and looks for, say, four bar staff for a Saturday, they don't start from scratch. They start with the people they already know.

On the producer's side there are three things you don't see:

- They can mark you as a **favourite**, and they have their own list where they'll find you without searching.
- They can leave a **private note** about your work.
- They can give you a **rating from 1 to 5** after each event.

They also see your history: which events you were at and how many hours you clocked at each one.

All of that is internal to the producer. You don't see it and you can't argue with it. The only thing you can do is what determines it: **the second event is decided during the first**.

## 1. Answer fast. Declining is also an answer

The offer reaches you by email with its own link, and that link **expires**. Meanwhile, on the other side, that position shows as pending: neither filled nor open.

If you accept, the place is filled. If you decline, it's open again and the producer can go and find someone else in time. Both answers help.

The one that doesn't help is not answering. An expired offer leaves the producer with a position they thought was nearly settled and that falls through just when there's no margin left. **Declining doesn't count against you. Disappearing does.**

And if you're about to let an offer expire without meaning to, you'll get a reminder before it lapses: use it, even if it's to say no.

## 2. Arrive at the call time

The call time is almost never the time the event opens. People are called earlier precisely so everything is ready when the public arrives.

Arriving at the call time (not five minutes later) is the habit that weighs most in whether you get called again, and it's also the easiest to keep. The day before your shift you'll get a reminder with the venue and the time, so there's no "I forgot" excuse. For the rest of the day, read [your first day working at an event](/en/blog/first-day-working-at-an-event).

## 3. Give early notice when you can't make it

It will happen. You get ill, something clashes, you can't get there.

The difference between a problem and a disaster is hours of notice. **A gap flagged in time gets covered; the same gap discovered with the doors open doesn't.** The shift reminder asks exactly that of you: let them know straight away on WhatsApp if you can't make it. How to do it well is in [how to cancel an event shift](/en/blog/how-to-cancel-an-event-shift).

Nobody remembers badly the person who gave notice in time. Everyone remembers the one who didn't show up and didn't answer.

## 4. Clock in and out

It looks like a formality and it's your backup. Clocking in records your real hours at the event, which is what nobody argues about later: you don't have to claim from memory and the producer doesn't have to pay from screenshots.

It also builds your history. Over time, your events worked and your clocked hours are concrete proof that you take this seriously.

## 5. Do well a job someone else would find hard

This is the one that pays off most in the medium term. The positions that make you hard to replace are the ones that add responsibility, certification, your own equipment or autonomy.

But before that there's something cheaper and quicker: **take the shifts nobody wants.** Setup, teardown, the early hours. There are more places, less competition and it's where people see you really work. Half the people who later coordinate a team started out unloading trucks. If you want to see that path, read [from event staff to team coordinator](/en/blog/from-event-staff-to-team-coordinator).

## 6. Keep your profile alive

If you've moved, added a trade, now have your own transport or can travel, update it. The filters used to build each event's crew are literal: area, weekend availability, willingness to travel, own transport and how much notice you need to take a job.

An out-of-date profile doesn't leave you out because of your work: it leaves you out before anyone sees it. If you never built it properly, [here's how](/en/blog/event-staff-profile-what-to-include).

## 7. Expand one position at a time, honestly

Adding neighbouring trades works: from barback to bartender, from loading to stagehand, from registration staff to scanner operator. Adding twenty trades you can't do doesn't: you get offers you can't fulfil, and then the note left on the other side is worse than if you'd never appeared.

Tick what you know, add what you're learning and explain it in your experience details.

## What we won't promise you

Being in the pool doesn't guarantee you'll be called, and accepting an event doesn't make you part of a permanent team: **you're confirmed for that event**, with set dates and hours. Event work is temporary and that's how it works.

What is true is this: in a trade where the number one problem is people who don't show up, being predictable is the best card you have. And it's free.

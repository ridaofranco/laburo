Requesting quotes looks like the easy part of putting an event together. You send a message, you wait, and you pick the cheapest. In practice, half the replies that come back aren't prices: they're questions. "For how many people?", "what time can we get in to set up?", "is the stage yours?". And when you finally have three numbers, they can't be compared, because each supplier quoted something different.

This guide is about avoiding both: requests that trigger questions back, and quotes that arrive impossible to compare.

## The details every request needs

Whether it's sound, catering, structures or transport, there are details any supplier needs to give you a serious price:

- **Date**, and if it's more than one day, all of the days.
- **Venue**, with address. A venue with a goods lift is not the same as a first floor reached by stairs.
- **Number of people.** For catering it defines everything; for sound or structures it defines the size.
- **Event hours**, from start to finish.
- **Setup and teardown times.** When they can get in to build and by when everything has to be out. It's often what moves the price most.
- **What you want included.** Transport, technician, service staff, crockery.

If any is missing, the supplier has two options: ask you (and you lose days) or assume (and quote something that isn't what you need). Both are worse than writing it properly the first time.

## Ask everyone for the same thing

It sounds obvious and it's what breaks most often. The first one gets the full brief, the second a voice note, the third "same as we discussed". Then you compare three prices that answer three different requests.

The rule is simple: **one text, the same, for everyone.** If after sending it you realise a detail was missing, add it for everyone, not just for whoever asked.

And one request, one job. If the client asked you for two different things (for example, two activations at different locations), it's better to request them separately: the suppliers for one aren't always the suppliers for the other, and two jobs in a single total can't be compared against anything.

## Ask for the price broken down

A closed package leaves nothing to move. If the client cuts a dinner or drops a screen, with a package you have to request everything again.

When what you're asking for has parts (each meal, each day, each service), ask for a price per line. That gives you two advantages:

1. You compare line against line, and see who is cheaper **at what**.
2. A supplier who only does one part can quote that part, instead of not replying.

Watch out for one trap: the lowest total isn't always the cheapest. If a quote leaves out one of the lines, its total covers less work. Always compare against a quote that covers everything you asked for.

## Compare in the same currency

In Argentina it's common for some suppliers to quote in pesos and others in US dollars. Ranking those numbers in the same list gives nonsense: 22,200 looks cheaper than 21,000,000 if you don't look at the symbol.

Before comparing, convert everything to the same currency at an exchange rate you set (and note which one you used), or compare each currency separately. The same goes for VAT: check whether the price includes it or not, and compare them on the same terms.

## What to ask before choosing

There are questions that apply to any trade and are worth putting in the request from the start:

- **What exactly is included in the price and what's charged separately?**
- **Until when is that price valid?** Does it change if the event moves date?
- **What payment method do you need and how much deposit do you ask to reserve?**

And then, the ones for each trade. Some examples:

| Trade | What to ask |
|---|---|
| Sound | What equipment is in the price, whether it comes with a technician and for how many hours, whether setup and transport are included |
| Catering | Price per head and what it includes, how many service staff, whether it covers coeliac, vegan and allergy needs, whether it needs a kitchen on site |
| Structures | Sizes and materials, whether setup and teardown are included, whether they provide signed structural calculations, how it behaves in wind and rain |

The kitchen question for catering deserves attention: if the service needs to cook on site and the venue has no kitchen, the event gets complicated and the price changes. More on that trade in [event catering business: get more quotes](/en/blog/event-catering-business-get-more-quotes).

One detail: ask, don't exclude. If you write "only quote if you have X", you'll lose quotes that might have worked anyway. Better to ask it and decide yourself when comparing.

## Set a deadline

Without a date, quoting is something for tomorrow. Say in the request until when you're accepting quotes, and make it a specific date, with a time. That way the replies arrive together and you compare everything at once, instead of choosing from whatever turns up.

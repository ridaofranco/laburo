At an event, the technical side is what nobody notices when it goes well and the first thing everyone notices when it fails. This guide is for anyone who wants to work as a sound or lighting technician: what happens at each stage, how to get into the trade and how to show it on your profile so you get called.

## What a technician does at an event

The job isn't just the time when the audience is there. It splits into four stages, and there is work in all of them.

### 1. Load-in and setup

You arrive hours before the audience. The kit is unloaded, each piece is placed according to the plan or whatever the person in charge says, cables are run, equipment is hung or stood up and everything is connected. Here you work shoulder to shoulder with setup crew, rigging and stage. If you want to understand that part of the day and how to look after yourself, it is in [event setup and teardown: how to work safely](/en/blog/event-setup-and-teardown-work-safely).

### 2. Testing

With everything connected, you test. In sound, every microphone and every source is checked, you adjust how it sounds at different points in the venue and you run a soundcheck with whoever is going to speak or play. In lighting, you check that every fixture responds, build the looks or scenes and go over the cues with whoever is running the event. This is the moment to find the problem, not during the show.

### 3. Operating

During the event, you operate: bringing levels up and down, taking cues, following the script. Communication with the stage and with the event's direction matters here. At many events the [stage manager](/en/blog/what-is-a-stage-manager-at-an-event) is the one calling cues, and you need to be alert to each one.

### 4. Load-out

When the audience leaves, the load-out begins. Everything is disconnected, each cable is coiled, each piece of kit goes into its case and gets loaded. It is the most tiring part because it comes at the end of a long day, and it is where the most gets lost or broken if it is rushed. Whoever stays until the last piece of kit is loaded is the one who gets called again.

## Sound and lighting are not the same

They share the logic of the four stages, but they ask for different things:

- **Sound** is about ears and listening. It requires understanding how sound behaves in a space, how a signal chain is built and how to fix a noise or feedback in seconds.
- **Lighting** is about eyes and programming. It requires understanding how a space looks, how looks and cues are built, and how fixtures are hung and focused.

At small events, sometimes one person does both. At large events they are separate teams, with their own roles.

## Other technical roles around you

During a setup you'll cross paths with other technical trades. At LABURO, the **technical (sound, lighting, video)** category has these roles:

- Sound technician
- Lighting technician
- Video and LED technician
- Switcher operator
- Stagehand
- Rigger
- Electrician
- Backline

Many technicians start as stagehands or in backline (the person who prepares and looks after instruments and equipment on stage) and move on to operating from there. Rigging and electrical work are specialisms in their own right: if you haven't trained for them, don't do them even if you are asked to in a rush.

## How to get into the trade

There is no single way in, but some paths come up again and again:

1. **As an assistant.** You help load, set up and take down alongside someone with experience. It is the most common school: you learn the kit, the timing and the vocabulary.
2. **At equipment rental companies.** Companies that rent out sound, lighting or screens need people to prep, load, set up and take down. It is physical work, but you are close to the kit the whole time.
3. **At technical production companies and venues.** Many venues and production companies have their own kit and need someone to set it up and run it on each date.
4. **Training separately.** Sound or lighting courses help you understand what you see during setup, but you only really learn the trade by working.

If you don't have event experience yet, [event jobs with no experience](/en/blog/event-jobs-with-no-experience) explains how to get your first dates.

## What's expected

What's valued most in a technician, beyond the kit you know:

- **Punctuality.** Setup runs to a schedule, and if you arrive late everything shifts.
- **Saying what you don't know.** If you don't know a desk or a piece of kit, say so before touching it. Nobody will fire you for asking; they will for breaking something.
- **Order.** Cables coiled the same way, cases labelled, everything in its place. The load-out shows who is tidy.
- **Calm when something fails.** During the event, the problem gets fixed without the audience finding out.
- **Staying until the end.** The load-out is part of the job, and it has to be in the hours you are offered.

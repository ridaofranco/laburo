At many events money doesn't go through the bar or the food stall: it goes through the cash desk. People buy tokens or tickets, and use them to order a drink or a burger. That is where the cashier works, and how the desk runs decides whether the queue moves, whether the bar gets stuck and whether the numbers add up at the end of the night.

This guide is for anyone who wants to work as a cashier at events: what the job involves, what you'll be asked for and what to check before you accept.

## Where events have a cash desk

Not every event has one. At a wedding or a corporate party the bar is usually prepaid and nobody charges anything. Where there is a cash desk:

- **Concerts, parties and festivals**, with a token or ticket desk for drinks and food.
- **Food fairs and design markets**, sometimes with a central desk and sometimes with each stall taking its own money.
- **Merchandise sales** at shows and sports events.
- **Door ticket sales**, when tickets are sold on the day.

Each one has its own rhythm. At a concert the queue forms all at once when doors open and again between bands. At a fair sales are more spread out, but the day is long.

## What you do on shift

**Before opening.** You are given a cash float and count it with the supervisor, note by note, and both of you write it down. You test the card reader and the QR payments, check there is paper if tickets are printed and find the price list. If something isn't working, say so then, not when twenty people are already waiting.

**During the event.** You take payment, give change and hand over the tokens or ticket. It sounds simple, but with a long queue, loud music and low light it gets harder. A fixed routine helps: say the total out loud, count the change in front of the customer and don't put the note away until you have given the change. If you make a mistake, write it down straight away and tell the supervisor.

**At close.** The till is counted: cash is counted, the card reader batch is closed and everything is checked against sales. Ideally the supervisor is there, just like at opening.

## What you'll be asked for most

- **Care with money.** Notes sorted by value, nothing loose on the counter and the till always in sight.
- **Speed without rushing.** The queue puts pressure on you, but wrong change costs more than ten seconds of waiting.
- **Knowing card readers and QR.** Less and less is paid in cash, and if the reader goes down you need to know what to do (and who to tell).
- **Calm with the public.** Someone who says they paid with a bigger note, someone who lost their tokens, someone who wants a refund. Arguing doesn't fix it: call the supervisor.
- **Never leaving the till alone.** If you need a break or to eat, ask for cover and close the till. Never leave it with "someone who was there".

## What to ask before accepting

Before you say yes, ask for:

1. **Start time and till closing time**, not just the event times. The cash count takes a while.
2. **How payments are taken**: cash, card, QR or all of them.
3. **Whether you'll be alone at the desk or with someone**, and who the supervisor is.
4. **What happens if there is a difference at close.** Every employer handles it differently and it is better to know beforehand.
5. **Whether there is cover for breaks** and how often.

## How to protect yourself

The cash desk is the only job at an event where a mistake shows up as a number. That is why it pays to have everything on record: the float counted at opening, voids written down as they happen, the final count done with someone beside you. It isn't distrust: it is what makes it possible to know why, if anything is short or over.

And think about getting home. Many desks close late, after the event has ended. If it's the middle of the night, plan your trip in advance: [getting home after a late night event](/en/blog/getting-home-after-a-late-night-event) has practical ideas.

## Cashier, bar or merchandise

Many people who work the cash desk also work the bar, merchandise sales or a booth. If you like dealing with the public and the pace of the night, the bar is the natural next step: see [event bartender jobs](/en/blog/event-bartender-jobs). If you prefer something calmer, merchandise sales at shows have shorter rushes and less money per transaction.

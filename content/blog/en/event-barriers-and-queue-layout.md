A badly laid-out queue shows before the event even starts: people crowded on the pavement, queue jumpers, an entrance that doesn't move and nobody knowing where the line ends. Barriers are what give that moment its shape. This guide is for production companies, businesses and organisers putting on a concert, a festival, a trade show or any event with an entrance: what types of barrier there are, how to plan the queue, how much to hire and who does what.

## What barriers are for

Three jobs, worth separating because they call for different barriers:

- **Organising queues.** Showing where people wait and which way they move.
- **Controlling entry.** Funnelling everyone to a point where tickets, registrations or bags are checked.
- **Separating areas.** General admission, premium area, press, backstage, technical zone, supplier exit.

## Types of barrier

| Type | What it's for | What it's not for |
|---|---|---|
| **Posts and tape / queue barriers** | Orderly queues indoors or at calm entrances, registration | Holding back pushing or an excited crowd |
| **Builders' or perimeter fencing** | Closing off perimeters, blocking access, marking out loading areas | Front of stage or areas with crowd pressure |
| **Crowd control barriers** | Front of stage, separating the audience at concerts and shows | Ordinary queues, where they are overkill and take up space |

The supplier knows which models they have and how much each can withstand. Your job is to tell them **what each section has to do** and what kind of crowd you expect. With that, they recommend the type.

## How to plan the queue route

Start with the plan, not with the barriers:

1. **Mark the entrances and exits.** Emergency exits are marked first and always stay clear.
2. **Place the checkpoints.** Where tickets are scanned, where bags are checked, where registration is. Each point is a bottleneck: the queue has to reach it one or two people at a time.
3. **Draw the queue backwards.** From the checkpoint outwards. If it doesn't fit in a straight line, lay it out in a zigzag with lanes going back and forth.
4. **Separate queues by type.** General tickets, premium, registered guests, press. Each with its own sign at the start.
5. **Leave a clear route.** For people with reduced mobility, for emergencies and for staff to move around without crossing the queue.
6. **Think about the exit.** People leaving shouldn't cross paths with people coming in.

To decide how many checkpoints to set up, the figures that matter are **how many people arrive together** and **how long each check takes**. With those two things you know how many people each point gets through per hour and how many points to open at the peak. The logic is the same as in [how many staff for an event](/en/blog/how-many-staff-for-an-event).

## How much barrier to hire

There is no fixed number per guest. It comes from the plan:

- **Measure each section.** During the site visit or on a scale plan: perimeters, frontages, area separations.
- **In the queue, count both sides.** A queue lane has barriers on both sides, and each turn of the zigzag adds sections.
- **Divide by the unit length.** The supplier tells you how long each barrier is. Total metres divided by unit length gives you the quantity.
- **Add spare units.** There is always a section that needs extending or an entrance that moves.
- **Ask for feet or ballast.** Outdoors, ask how they are secured and whether wind or terrain changes anything.

Send the supplier the plan with the measurements and ask them to confirm the quantity. To know what to include in that request, see [how to request quotes from event suppliers](/en/blog/how-to-request-quotes-from-event-suppliers).

## Delivery and collection

Agree in writing:

- **Delivery day and time**, with time to set up before the public arrives.
- **Where the truck comes in** and where it unloads.
- **Who sets up**: whether the supplier builds to your plan or only delivers.
- **Collection time**, which is usually at night or the next day.
- **What happens with damaged or missing units.**

Before the doors open, someone from coordination walks the setup with the plan in hand.

## Who manages the queue

Barriers don't organise anything on their own. **Access control staff** are what make the queue work: they tell people which queue they belong in, check tickets or registrations, open and close points according to the pace and tell coordination if the queue gets out of hand. At events with large crowds, **security** is usually added, which is a different role with different licences; in Argentina it is explained in [private security at events in Buenos Aires](/en/blog/private-security-at-events-buenos-aires).

If the event is a show, see [concert and live show staffing](/en/blog/concert-and-live-show-staffing), and if it is outdoors, [outdoor festival staffing](/en/blog/outdoor-festival-staffing).

## Safety and evacuation

- **No barrier blocks an exit**, an evacuation route or ambulance access.
- **Sections that close off an exit in normal use** must be able to open quickly, with someone assigned to them.
- **Front of stage**: if the crowd pushes, crowd control barriers with staff behind them, never a lightweight barrier.
- **Signage**: exits must be visible from the queue.

The specific requirements (number and width of exits, evacuation plans, permits) depend on the venue, the size of the crowd and the local authority. Confirm them with the venue manager, whoever handles health and safety and the local authority before finalising the plan.

## Common mistakes

- **Ordering barriers by gut feeling** with no plan or measurements.
- **Using builders' fencing at the front of the stage.**
- **A single checkpoint** for the whole crowd at the peak.
- **Queues without signs**, where people join the wrong line.
- **Barriers blocking an exit** because "it was only for a while".
- **Nobody in charge of the queue**, just the barriers.

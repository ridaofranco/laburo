On the other side there's no mysterious algorithm. There's a producer who needs, say, four bar staff in the city of Córdoba for a Saturday night, who opens the pool, filters and looks at cards.

Everything below is about making sure you show up on that screen, and that when you do, they open your profile.

## First: the fields that are filters

This is the part almost nobody knows. **Several fields in your profile aren't decorative: they're the filters used to build the staff for each event.** If they're empty or filled in badly, you don't appear on the list, however much experience you have.

**The positions you apply for.** They're chosen from a specific list, organised by families: bar and hospitality, access and registration, production and logistics, sound, lighting and video tech, audiovisual, guest service and experience, sales and commercial, cleaning and maintenance, health and safety, transport, and other profiles. The producer searches by role, so if you didn't tick the position, you don't exist for that search.

**Province and city.** Searches filter by province and look up by city. A profile without a city gets lost in half the searches, because many are made precisely to avoid calling in someone who'll be two hours away at 5 a.m.

**Availability.** Three simple ticks, and all three are used as filters: available at weekends, willing to travel and own transport. Most events are at weekends and at night; if you're available, tick it. And if you have a car or a motorbike, tick that too: it's a real tie-breaker for shifts that end in the early hours.

**How much notice you need to take a job.** The options are available immediately, with 24 to 48 hours' notice, with a week's notice, or to be arranged. This field matters most when someone needs to fill a position fast. If you can start tomorrow, say so.

**Where you want to work.** As well as your city, you can say whether you're interested in working in your country of residence, in Argentina, in the rest of Latin America, in Spain or Europe, in the United States or Canada, in the Middle East, or anywhere. And your right-to-work situation, which is the first thing checked for events outside your country.

## Second: what's seen before your profile is opened

The results list doesn't show your full profile. It shows a card with **your name, your main trade, up to three of your trades, your city and province, and an experience signal**.

That signal comes, in this order, from the number of events you've worked, or from your range of years of experience, or (if you filled in neither) from a plain "experienced".

It's a small detail with a big consequence: **if you don't fill in the number of events or the years, your card shows the weakest of the three signals.** Next to a card that says "12 events", you lose before anyone has read a word of yours.

Another detail: the first trade you tick is the one shown as your main trade. Order them with how you want to be seen in mind.

## Third: the field that decides, "tell us about your experience"

When your profile is opened, people see your experience in detail, your availability, your situation, your links and your CV. The field that weighs most is the free-text experience: events, roles and brands you've worked with.

There's only one rule: **specific beats generic, every time.**

Generic: "I have hospitality experience and I'm good with people."

Specific: "Two seasons behind the bar at parties of 500 to 2,000 people. Bar setup and close, classic cocktails, working with order tickets. I've also done registration at conferences: QR scanning and badge handout."

The second one doesn't exaggerate any more than the first. It just says precisely what the person did, which is exactly what a producer needs to decide where to put you.

Three things worth including in that text:

- **What kind of event** (party, conference, concert, corporate, sports, wedding).
- **What size**, even if approximate.
- **What exactly you did**, not the job title.

And if your field has something formal that sets you apart (a certification, a licence, a permit), put it in. In health, security and transport roles that's not decoration.

## The CV and the links

You can upload your CV and a good part of the form fills itself in; then you review and correct anything that came out wrong. **Check it anyway**: autofill saves time, but a wrong detail you didn't fix is a filter you won't show up in.

If your role is audiovisual (photo, camera, drone, editing, streaming), add your portfolio. There your work is shown, not described.

## Contact details: obvious, and yet

Offers arrive **by email**. An email address with a typo is a profile that never hears about anything.

Enter your email and phone or WhatsApp correctly. And check your spam folder in the first few days, because offers have an expiry date: the link expires, and an expired offer can no longer be accepted.

## The five mistakes that make a profile invisible

1. **Not entering your city.** It takes you out of area searches, which are almost all of them.
2. **Ticking twenty trades.** You don't appear in more useful searches; you appear in the ones that are no use to you.
3. **Leaving your experience as two generic lines.** It's the most read field and the least completed.
4. **Not entering years or number of events.** Your card starts with the weakest signal.
5. **Not replying.** Declining an offer doesn't count against you. Not replying is what makes offers stop coming.

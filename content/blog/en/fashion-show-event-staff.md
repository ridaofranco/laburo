A fashion show lasts twenty minutes on the runway and twelve hours of work. What the audience sees is the looks going out on time, an entrance with no queues and a tidy venue. What they don't see is the people holding all that together since the morning: backstage, at the door and among the guests. This guide is for production companies, agencies and brands that have to put together the operational staff for a fashion show or fashion event.

## Two worlds: backstage and front of house

It helps to think of the team in two parts that hardly cross paths.

**Backstage.** It's the most cramped and demanding place at the event. The creative team works there (dressers, hairdressers, make-up artists, who usually come from the brand or designer), along with the operational staff who support them:

- **Production assistant.** Keeps the space in order, follows the schedule and sorts out whatever is missing.
- **Runner.** Back and forth: missing garments, accessories, water, food, errands between backstage and front of house. What exactly they do is in [what does an event runner do](/en/blog/what-does-an-event-runner-do).
- **Stagehand.** Builds and moves the physical things: clothes rails, mirrors, platforms, runway set pieces.
- **Stage manager.** If the show has several exits, music and lighting on cue, someone has to call them. It's a judgement role, explained in [what is a stage manager at an event](/en/blog/what-is-a-stage-manager-at-an-event).

**Front of house.** This is the guest's experience, from arrival to departure:

- **Registration staff and Access control.** List, badges, wristbands, door. In fashion there are press guests, buyers, influencers and members of the public with different seating, so registration has to tell them apart without slowing down.
- **Cloakroom.** In winter, or with a crowd coming from another event, it can produce the longest queue of the night. How to organise it is in [how to run an event cloakroom](/en/blog/how-to-run-an-event-cloakroom).
- **Hostess, Host and Guest coordinator.** They welcome people, walk each one to their seat and make sure the front row is the one it's meant to be.
- **Cleaning staff.** Before doors open and during the cocktail reception, if there is one.

## How to size the team

The numbers come from the pace of each post, not from the total number of guests. Three questions per area:

1. **Front of house: how many people arrive in the half hour before the show?** Almost everyone arrives together. That peak sets how many registration points and how many people in the cloakroom.
2. **Backstage: how many exits and changes are there?** More looks and more quick changes need more operational hands, as long as there's room. A backstage full of people with nothing to do also jams things up.
3. **How many hours does each block last?** To plan reliefs and breaks.

**Example with assumed numbers** (just to show the maths): you want to register 300 guests in the 30 minutes before the show, and one person with a list and scanner registers about 2 people per minute. That gives 5 registration points at the peak, plus one access control person to handle the cases who aren't on the list. After the show, part of that team can move to the cloakroom or the exit. Measure the real pace at the dress rehearsal and adjust.

The general logic is in [how many staff do you need for an event](/en/blog/how-many-staff-for-an-event).

## Long rehearsal days

What's most underestimated at a fashion show is the length of the day. It usually runs in this order:

- **Setup** of the runway, lighting, sound and backstage.
- **Technical rehearsal** (lighting, music, exits without models).
- **Rehearsal with models** and wardrobe fitting.
- **Doors opening** and registration.
- **The show.**
- **Cocktails or departure**, and **teardown**.

Not everyone needs to be there from the first block. Operational backstage staff come in with the setup or the rehearsal; front of house can arrive a few hours before doors open, with time to get to know the venue, the list and the seating. That way you don't pay for hours of people waiting and nobody arrives tired for the moment that matters.

Three rules that prevent problems:

- **The full schedule, written into the offer**, with the real arrival time and an estimated finishing time. In fashion, rehearsals slip: say up front what happens if the day runs long.
- **Meals and breaks planned** for anyone who's there from the morning. Nobody backstage has time to pop out and buy food.
- **Overlapping reliefs** if any post runs from start to finish.

If the event spans several days (a fashion week or a trade fair with shows), the block-based schedule is in [shift planning for long events](/en/blog/shift-planning-for-long-events).

## What profile to ask for

- **Discretion backstage.** There are people changing, expensive garments and a lot of tension. Calm, quiet and no photos.
- **Judgement at the door.** There's always someone who isn't on the list. Saying no without causing a scene is part of the job.
- **A look that fits the brand.** The dress code for front-of-house staff is set by the brand and has to be in the call. Ideas in [event staff dress code](/en/blog/event-staff-dress-code).
- **Punctuality.** A fashion show doesn't wait.

For reception, the profile is developed in [corporate event hostess and reception](/en/blog/corporate-event-hostess-and-reception).

## Common mistakes

- **Calling everyone at the same time** and leaving front of house waiting six hours for the rehearsal.
- **Not sending the guest list and seating** to registration before the day.
- **A backstage with nobody keeping order**, leaving the creative team to sort out operational tasks.
- **A cloakroom with one person** on a cold night.
- **Not saying what happens if the day runs long.** It's the most common argument at the end of a fashion show.

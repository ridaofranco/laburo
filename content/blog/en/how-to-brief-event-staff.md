The briefing is the short meeting with the whole staff before doors open: what the event is, what each person does, who to ask and what not to do. It lasts 10 to 20 minutes and, done well, prevents most of the mistakes of the day. This guide is for production companies, agencies and businesses putting a team together for an event who want that team to start work knowing the same things.

## Why the briefing matters so much

At an event, a good part of the staff has never worked together. They come from different places, they know their trade but not this event, this venue or these people. The briefing is the only moment when everyone hears the same thing at the same time. Whatever isn't said there, each person solves their own way, and it shows at the door, at the bar and in how a guest gets answered.

## When to run it

With the full team on site and already changed, but with time to spare before opening. That is why call time is always earlier than doors: the briefing, getting to the station, testing what needs testing and being ready when the first guest walks in.

If the event is long and has shifts, every incoming shift needs its own short briefing. The people arriving at eight in the evening weren't there at two in the afternoon. How to plan those handovers is in [shift planning for long events](/en/blog/shift-planning-for-long-events).

## What it has to cover

An order that works:

1. **What the event is and who is coming.** A product launch for clients isn't handled like an employee end of year party. Say who the guests are and what the client expects.
2. **The run of show.** Opening, key moments (speeches, show, dinner, close) and finish time. Each key moment changes what someone has to do.
3. **Who does what.** Role by role, with names. Every person should hear their name and their task.
4. **Who is in charge and who to ask.** "Any question, go to her; if you can't find her, go to him." Point those people out so everyone recognises them.
5. **Breaks and meals.** When, where and how to ask for cover. If it isn't said, people either don't rest or leave their station without telling anyone.
6. **What to do if something happens.** Someone feels unwell, a guest becomes difficult, something breaks, the power goes out. You don't need a manual: it is enough to know who to tell and where the exits are.
7. **What not to do.** Phone visible in hand, eating or drinking at the station, answering something you don't know instead of asking, leaving the station empty.

## How to run it so it sticks

- **Short and standing.** 10 to 20 minutes. If it drags on, people switch off.
- **On site, not in a separate room.** If you can, walk the stations as you talk: the door, the bar, the cloakroom, the emergency exit.
- **With names, not roles.** "Martina, you're on the door with the list" sticks better than "front of house at the entrance".
- **In writing too.** Send a summary beforehand with times, address, dress code and who they report to, then repeat it in the briefing. What people read and then hear stays with them.
- **With room for questions.** End with "any questions?" and actually wait. Questions at that moment save you problems at eleven at night.

## What to send in writing beforehand

The briefing works better if the team already arrives knowing the basics. A message before the event with:

- The exact address, which entrance to use and call time.
- What to wear (if in doubt, see the [event staff dress code](/en/blog/event-staff-dress-code)).
- What to bring: ID, a charger, whatever the role needs.
- The name and phone number of the coordinator.

## Who runs it

The person coordinating the staff that day, because that is who the team will look for when something happens. If someone from production or the client wants to say a few words, let them go first and keep it brief; the coordinator closes with the operational part. If the team is large and nobody has that role, see [when you need an event staff coordinator](/en/blog/when-you-need-an-event-staff-coordinator).

## Common mistakes

- **Running it with doors already open**, half the team at their stations and the other half listening.
- **Talking only in general terms** and never saying who does what.
- **Not introducing the coordinator**, so nobody knows who to turn to.
- **Forgetting latecomers** or the night shift.
- **Not saying what happens if someone doesn't show up.** If it happens, keep [what to do when event staff don't show up](/en/blog/event-staff-no-show-what-to-do) at hand.

After the event, what you saw in the briefing and during the day helps you [evaluate the staff](/en/blog/how-to-evaluate-event-staff) and know who to call again.

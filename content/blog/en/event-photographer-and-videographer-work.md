Photography and video are among the few event trades that leave something lasting: what you capture is what the client will look at afterwards, and what they will show others. This guide is for anyone who wants to work as an event photographer or videographer: how it works at LABURO, what changes between a social event and a corporate one, what to agree beforehand and how to show your work.

## Two ways to be on LABURO

At LABURO, photography and video appear in two different ways, and it helps to know which one is yours.

**As staff.** If you are hired by the day or by the event to join a team, you fill in your profile and mark roles from the **audiovisual** category:

- Photographer
- Camera operator
- Drone operator
- Editor
- Streaming

There is no role in the catalogue called "filmmaker": if you shoot and edit, mark camera operator and editor, and explain it in your experience.

**As a supplier.** If you run your own service (with your own kit, your own editing and your own way of delivering), you list yourself as a supplier and choose the **photography and video** category, or **streaming and broadcasting** if you stream live. People searching for suppliers filter by those categories. If you do both, add one service for each, so you appear in both categories.

You can be on LABURO both ways: some people work as camera operators for others and, separately, sell their own coverage.

## Social and corporate events

The camera is the same, but the job changes.

**Social events** (weddings, quinceañeras, birthdays). The focus is on people and moments that don't happen twice: the entrance, the toast, the dance, the family. Before the event, go over with whoever hires you which moments can't be missed and who the key people are. If you want to see how the rest of the team is put together at these events, see [how many staff for a wedding](/en/blog/how-many-staff-for-a-wedding) and [staffing a quinceañera party](/en/blog/staffing-a-quinceanera-party).

**Corporate events** (launches, conferences, company parties). There is usually a brand, a script, speakers and a communications team asking for specific things: a photo of each speaker, the logo visible, audience shots, testimonials. They often need material the same day for social media or the press. Ask beforehand which deliverables are needed on the spot and which come later. [Product launch event staff](/en/blog/product-launch-event-staff) and [conference and convention staff](/en/blog/conference-and-convention-staff) explain how those events are organised.

In both cases, you work alongside the technical crew, the stage and production. Knowing where you'll be, where you can move and who to tell if you need to get through is part of the job.

## What to agree before the event

Most problems with photography and video don't show up on the day: they show up afterwards, when the client was expecting something else. To avoid that, agree in writing, even if it is just a message:

1. **Hours of coverage.** From what time to what time, and what happens if the event runs over.
2. **What is delivered.** How many edited photos, which video pieces (highlights, full event, short pieces for social media), in what format and by what means.
3. **In how many days.** The delivery deadline for the edited material and, if there is one, for a preview.
4. **Which moments are essential.** The list of what can't be missed.
5. **What you need from the venue.** Access, somewhere to leave your kit, power to charge batteries, food if it is a long day.
6. **Who can use the material and for what.** Whether you can use it in your portfolio, whether the client can use it in advertising or only internally, and for how long.

On that last point: usage rights depend on what is agreed, and there is no single answer. What matters is that it is said beforehand and in writing. If the job is big or involves a brand, and you are sent a contract, check it with a professional before signing.

If you are asked for a quote, [how to reply to an event quote request](/en/blog/how-to-reply-to-an-event-quote-request) explains what to answer.

## On the day of the event

- **Arrive early.** Get to know the venue, the light and the routes before people arrive.
- **Bring backup.** Spare batteries and cards. Kit that runs out of battery at the key moment can't be fixed.
- **Introduce yourself to production.** Make sure they know who you are and where you'll be.
- **Don't block anyone.** Not the audience, not other cameras, not the stage.

For what to bring in general, see [what to bring when working an event](/en/blog/what-to-bring-when-working-an-event).

## How to show your work

Whoever hires photography or video wants to see work similar to theirs before deciding.

**On your staff profile.** There is a **portfolio** field and a **LinkedIn** field, and you can upload your CV. In the free-text experience field, write what kind of events you have covered and what you did: "I've covered corporate events and weddings, photo and video, with my own editing" says much more than "I have experience".

**On your supplier profile.** The profile has no photo gallery (that is for venues). Your work is seen through your **Instagram** and your **website**, which go in your details. In the description of each service, state the hours of coverage, how many photos or pieces you deliver and in how many days: it is what you'll be asked anyway. The full guide is in [event supplier profile: what to include to get quote requests](/en/blog/event-supplier-profile-get-quote-requests).

In both cases: the link should lead to real event work, kept separate from your personal stuff, and the material you show should be material you have permission to show.

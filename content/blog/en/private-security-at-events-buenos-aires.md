If you're putting on an event in Buenos Aires City (the Argentine capital, known locally as CABA) and you're adding security, putting someone at the door isn't enough. Private security is an activity regulated by the City, with licensed companies, registered staff and obligations for whoever hires them too. This guide summarises what the City's rules say, based on the official text, so you know what to ask for.

**Note:** this is general information to help you find your way, not legal advice. For your specific case, check with the authority or a professional.

## Private security is not the same as admission control

At the door of an event, two things that are often confused tend to coexist:

- **Private security:** surveillance, custody and protection of people and property. In the City it's provided by a company or person **licensed** by the City government, with **guards** registered on its roll.
- **Admission and stay control:** the staff who decide who gets in and who stays at a show, regulated by Argentina's national Law 26,370 and its decree, with its own licence card. We explain it in [the guide to Law 26,370](/en/blog/argentina-law-26370-door-staff).

And separately there's **access control and registration** (validating tickets, handing out wristbands or badges, directing people), which is neither of the two. Book VI itself says that Law 26,370 applies to anything it doesn't cover: the rules overlap, but they're not the same.

## Which rules apply in the City

Buenos Aires City's private security rules are in **Book VI of Law 5688**, which regulates surveillance, custody and protection services for people and property provided by private parties based in the City or working within its territory. The law defines these services as complementary to public security and limited to deterrence, protecting people and safeguarding property.

Among the services without firearms, the law expressly includes **custody and door work at dance venues, bars and live shows**, and surveillance at fixed sites open to the public. Weapons are banned at those sites.

Oversight is in the hands of the **Directorate General of Private Security and Asset Custody**, which licenses providers and keeps the register of companies and their staff.

## What the law requires

### Of the company

- **A current licence for the activity.** Providing services without a current licence, or at sites not declared to the authority, is prohibited. The licence is granted for no more than two years and must be renewed.
- **Civil liability insurance** that expressly covers private security work.
- **A technical director** with a university or higher-education degree in security, who is jointly liable with the company.
- **Official registers** of staff, incidents and shifts (one per site, listing the staff assigned).
- **Uniforms and vehicles** clearly different from those of the official forces.

In addition, the company can't provide services in public spaces without express authorisation. If your event is on the street or in a square, ask about that first.

### Of the guard

Each guard must be registered and meet requirements such as having completed secondary school, being of legal age, holding a certificate of physical and psychological fitness, having the qualifying technical training and having no convictions for intentional crimes. For large-scale events and nightclubs, a 2024 order requires providers to have them take a specialisation course at the Higher Institute of Public Security first.

And the guard must **carry their badge**, show it when the authority asks and, in places open to the public, always wear it visibly. The badge shows at a minimum:

- the company name,
- a photo,
- first and last name,
- registration number,
- expiry date.

The uniform must also visibly show their name and registration number.

### Of you, the one hiring

This is what's most often overlooked: the law also places obligations on the **client**, meaning whoever hires. Before hiring, you must ask the company for a **certificate proving its licence**. And you must be able to show the **current contract** if the authority asks for it during an inspection.

## How to check the company is licensed

1. **Ask for the licence certificate** and check the expiry date and the type of activity.
2. **Check the public registers.** The law says the City publishes on its official website the list of licensed providers and each one's staff, and that anyone can request information from those registers without giving a reason. The authority can also certify a licence on request.
3. **If in doubt, ask the Directorate General of Private Security and Asset Custody** of the City government.
4. **On the day, look at the badges.** Each person should have theirs visible and the company name should match the one you hired.

## What to ask for in writing

Before closing the deal, ask by email or in the contract for:

- A current **licence certificate** for the type of service you'll use.
- The **list of staff** assigned, with the name and registration number of each guard.
- **Proof of the large-scale events course** if your event is one.
- **Proof of the company's civil liability insurance**.
- The **name of the supervisor** who will be on site and a direct phone number.
- **What the team does and doesn't do**: where each person stands, hours and who they answer to.
- **The signed contract**, which you'll keep in case you're asked for it.

In your request, separate security from access or registration. If the same person has to scan tickets and handle an incident, they'll do both badly.

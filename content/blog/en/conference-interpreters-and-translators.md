A conference with guests from abroad needs to solve language in several places at once: the stage, the printed materials, the registration desk and the delegation arriving at the airport. Each one calls for a different profile, and the most common mistake is hiring "a translator" to cover everything. This guide is for organisers: which profile to ask for, how many people you need and what you have to prepare so the work goes well.

## Four jobs that get confused

### Written translation

This is work on texts: the programme, presentations, leaflets, signage, certificates, the content of the event website. It is done beforehand (or afterwards, if there are proceedings or final materials) and doesn't require being in the room. A translator needs time: a programme that arrives the night before gets translated in a rush, and it shows.

### Simultaneous interpreting

The interpreter works from a booth while the speaker talks, and the audience hears the version in their language through headsets. It is the option for plenaries, panels and any session where the pace can't stop. It requires a booth, transmission equipment and receivers for the audience: you can't improvise it with a handheld microphone.

### Consecutive interpreting

The speaker talks for a stretch, pauses, and the interpreter repeats it in the other language, usually taking notes next to the speaker. It doesn't need a booth, but it lengthens each speech, because everything is said twice. It works for a short welcome speech, a press conference, a bilateral meeting or a technical visit.

### Bilingual escorts for delegations

This is the profile that accompanies a group of visitors: meets them, guides them around the venue, answers practical questions and acts as a bridge in informal conversations. It isn't conference interpreting and doesn't replace the booth. It is often covered by a reception profile with good language skills, like those described in [corporate event hostess and reception](/en/blog/corporate-event-hostess-and-reception).

## Why simultaneous interpreting is done in pairs

Simultaneous interpreting requires a level of concentration that can't be sustained for hours on end. That is why the professional standards of AIIC (the International Association of Conference Interpreters) state that, as a general rule, a team has **at least two interpreters per language and per booth**, and that an interpreter shouldn't work alone in a booth without a colleague available to take over.

The same standards say the number should go up when the day is long, when several languages are combined or when the conference is technical or scientific and requires a lot of preparation. In practice, the two interpreters in the booth take turns: while one interprets, the other rests, follows the thread, looks up a term or notes down figures and names.

For your planning, that means:

- **Count pairs, not individuals.** One booth per target language, with its own team of interpreters.
- **If there are parallel sessions,** each room with simultaneous interpreting needs its own booth and its own team.
- **Ask the interpreter** how many colleagues they need given the length and the subject. They are the best judge of how to size their team.

## What to ask the interpreter and what to give them beforehand

The quality of interpreting depends heavily on preparation. AIIC states that working documents and texts read at the conference should be sent to interpreters in advance, and that a briefing should be requested where appropriate. What to send:

1. **The programme** with times, rooms and who speaks in each block.
2. **Each speaker's presentation**, even if they are drafts.
3. **Texts that will be read out** (speeches, statements, written papers).
4. **A glossary:** proper names, job titles, acronyms, brands and technical terms from the sector, with their version in the other language if you have it.
5. **Background material:** previous editions of the conference, reports, press releases.
6. **A contact** for questions during the event.

And what to ask whoever you hire: which language combination they work in, in what kind of events and subjects they have experience, whether they work in simultaneous, consecutive or both, and whether they put together their own booth team.

## The booth is a different supplier

Interpreters bring the skill; the equipment comes from a technical supplier. That includes the booths, the interpreters' consoles, the transmission system and the receivers and headsets for the audience. AIIC's standards also call for good conditions of sound, visibility and comfort, and a direct view of the speaker and the room.

Some things to coordinate:

- **Where the booth goes:** with a view of the stage and the screen, and away from doors and walkways.
- **The audio feed:** the room's sound technician has to send the booth a clean signal from every microphone.
- **The receivers:** who hands them out, who collects them at the end and how you check they come back. That usually falls to registration; [what conference registration staff do](/en/blog/conference-registration-staff-role) explains how that desk is organised.

To know what to ask the technical suppliers, see [how to request quotes from event suppliers](/en/blog/how-to-request-quotes-from-event-suppliers).

## Common mistakes

- **Hiring a single interpreter for a day of simultaneous interpreting.** It goes against professional standards and quality drops as the hours go by.
- **Sending the material at the last minute.** The interpreter prepares the vocabulary beforehand, not in the booth.
- **Asking a written translator to interpret live,** or the other way round. They are different trades.
- **Using consecutive interpreting in a long plenary.** It doubles the time and breaks the programme.
- **Forgetting the receivers** in the budget or in the logistics of handing them out and getting them back.
- **Not telling the booth about changes of speaker or schedule.** The interpreter needs to know who is speaking and about what.

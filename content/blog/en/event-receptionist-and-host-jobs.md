At most events, the first face a guest sees isn't the organiser's: it is reception's. That person has the list in hand, knows who should come in through which door and is the one who raises the alert when someone arrives who can't be kept waiting. This guide explains what a receptionist or host does at an event, how it differs from similar roles, what's expected and how to add it to your profile.

## What a receptionist or host does

The name changes with the event (receptionist, host, greeter), but the job revolves around four tasks.

- **Greeting.** Saying hello to each person who arrives, welcoming them and making them feel expected. It sounds like little, but it is what sets the tone of the event from the first minute.
- **Handling the list.** Finding the guest, marking them as arrived and sorting out the unclear cases: a misspelt name, a plus-one who isn't listed, someone who says they are invited and doesn't appear. Who authorises those exceptions is decided by the organiser, not by reception.
- **Directing.** Telling people where to go: the room, their table, the cloakroom, the press area or a speaker's room. At a wedding or a party it may be the seating plan; at a corporate event, each company's area.
- **Looking after VIPs.** Special guests, speakers, officials or people from the brand. They are received separately, escorted to their place and coordination is told they have arrived. There is often a separate list and an arrival point of their own.

The host is also the first to spot a problem: a queue building up, someone feeling unwell, an annoyed guest. They don't solve everything, but they know who to call.

## How it differs from hostess and registration staff

These roles overlap, and it is worth separating them in a brief:

- **Registration staff:** at the door during the entrance rush. They validate sign-ups, scan and hand out badges. Their work is fast and focused. We explain it in [what conference registration staff do](/en/blog/conference-registration-staff-role).
- **Hostess:** usually works inside throughout the day, directing people to rooms and areas, sharing the agenda and keeping changeovers orderly. It is covered in [corporate event hostess and reception](/en/blog/corporate-event-hostess-and-reception).
- **Receptionist or host:** at the arrival point. Greets, handles the list, decides what gets passed on and escorts special guests.

At a large conference they can be three separate teams. At a company party or a wedding, reception sometimes does all three. Both ways work as long as the hours and tasks are spelled out beforehand.

## What's expected

- **Friendly but firm manners.** You need to be able to say "I can't find you on the list, let me check" with the same kindness to the three hundredth person. No arguing and no making up answers.
- **Judgement.** Knowing what you solve yourself and what you pass to coordination. If you don't know something, "let me check and get back to you" is better than sending someone to the wrong place.
- **Keeping the list tidy.** Mark each person as they arrive, not from memory. A badly kept list means an empty seat that looks taken or a VIP nobody went to meet.
- **A memory for names and faces.** Recognising who has already come in, remembering a speaker's name, knowing who the brand's person is. You train it by going over the list before the event.
- **Punctuality.** Reception is called before opening to walk the venue, receive the list and learn where everything is.
- **A well-kept appearance.** The event sets the clothing: a provided uniform, all black, formal in one colour. What is assessed is neatness and manner, not physical looks. The details are in [event staff dress code](/en/blog/event-staff-dress-code).

## Languages as a plus

At events with guests from abroad (conferences, launches with foreign press, meetings of companies with offices in other countries) English is often requested, and sometimes Portuguese. You don't need to speak perfectly: what matters is giving a clear direction, understanding a question and knowing who to pass it to.

If you speak another language, make it visible. Put it on your CV and in the experience text on your profile, with something specific: "I ran reception at a conference in English" says more than "intermediate English".

## Before each event

- **Ask for the list in good time** and go over it: difficult names, special guests, plus-ones.
- **Ask who authorises exceptions** and how you reach them (phone, radio, a fixed point).
- **Walk the venue** before it opens: room, toilets, cloakroom, emergency exit, VIP area.
- **Confirm the closing time.** Reception usually stays until the last guests arrive and, sometimes, until they leave.

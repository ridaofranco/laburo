At an outdoor event rain isn't a surprise: it's one of the two things that can happen. What turns it into a problem is the team finding out on the spot what they have to do, what they'll be paid and where to take shelter. This guide is for people who hire: what changes for staff if it rains and what to decide beforehand so the day doesn't fall apart.

## Who decides, and when

The first thing to put in writing isn't what clothes to bring, but who decides. At an outdoor event there are three possible decisions: **carry on**, **pause** or **cancel**. Each one needs a named person with a phone number.

Some decisions aren't yours. If there are marquees, stages or structures, whoever builds them defines the conditions they can be used in, and that should be in their quote (we cover it for suppliers in [event tent and structure rental](/en/blog/event-tent-and-structure-rental-get-quotes)). If there's a local authority or a site manager, their instruction rules.

To check the forecast in Argentina, the source is the **National Meteorological Service (SMN)**, the agency responsible for issuing warnings about weather events. Its early warning system has three levels (yellow, orange and red) depending on the intensity of the event and gives up to 72 hours' notice, on maps by area. Decide in advance what you do with the team at each level, and check the exact area of the event. Outside Argentina, use your country's official weather service.

Add two fixed decision points: **one the day before**, to confirm the call or move it to another date, and **one before the team leaves home**, so nobody travels to an event that isn't going to happen.

## What happens with shifts and pay

It's the question that gets argued most and written down least. Before you call people in, define:

- **If it's cancelled before the call time:** what is paid, if anything, and how much notice is given.
- **If it's cancelled with the team already on site:** what part of the shift is paid.
- **If it's paused:** whether waiting time counts as shift time and where people wait.
- **If it moves to another date:** whether the person keeps their spot and what happens if they can't make that day.

All of that has to be in the offer the person accepts, not in a voice note on the day. How to organise team payments in general is in [how to organise event staff payments](/en/blog/how-to-organise-event-staff-payments).

If the event pauses and runs over, review the schedule: a shift that was meant to last six hours can end up lasting nine. Relief and overlaps are covered in [shift planning for long events](/en/blog/shift-planning-for-long-events).

## Clothing and what the team should bring

If the event is outdoors, the call has to say so plainly. Ask for:

- **A raincoat or waterproof jacket**, or make clear the organisers provide it.
- **Closed shoes** that don't slip. Wet canvas trainers for six hours are a problem.
- **A change of clothes** (at least socks and a T-shirt) in a bag.
- **A bag for their phone.**

If the organisers provide the uniform, think about how it looks wet and whether there's an alternative. The rest of the kit is in [what to bring when working an event](/en/blog/what-to-bring-when-working-an-event), and uniforms in [event staff dress code](/en/blog/event-staff-dress-code).

## Tasks that change and tasks that appear

With rain, every position's work changes, and tasks appear that weren't in any brief:

| Position | What changes with rain |
|---|---|
| Check-in and access | Protect lists, scanners and badges; reorganise the queue under cover; entry becomes slower |
| Bars | Cover equipment, protect the till and electrics, relocate if the bar is exposed |
| Runners | Bring raincoats, bags and towels; move material that can't get wet |
| Setup and stagehands | Cover technical equipment, check cables and tarpaulins, follow whatever the structure builder says |
| Cleaning | Dry and signpost floors, mud at entrances and toilets: it's the position that grows most |
| Cloakroom | Take umbrellas and raincoats if there's a covered area |

Assign those tasks beforehand, by name: the plan B doesn't work if everyone waits for someone to say what to do. For cleaning, read [cleaning staff for an event](/en/blog/cleaning-staff-for-an-event).

## Where the team takes shelter

As well as where the public takes shelter, define **where the staff take shelter**: a covered place, known to everyone and marked on the plan, for breaks and for waiting if things pause. That's where the water, the food and the coordinator need to be. If there's a thunderstorm, nobody stays at an exposed post waiting for instructions.

## How to inform the team

There are three moments:

1. **In the call:** that it's outdoors, what to bring and what happens to the shift if it rains.
2. **The day before:** confirmation or change, in writing and to everyone at once. Ask each person to confirm they've read it.
3. **During the event:** one person per area who passes down the decision. If there are radios, who has them; if not, a meeting point people return to when the plan changes.

If someone can't come because of a date change, you need to be able to replace them quickly: that's in [event staff no-show: what to do](/en/blog/event-staff-no-show-what-to-do).

Everything else the event needs (heat, positions, hydration) is covered in [outdoor festival staffing](/en/blog/outdoor-festival-staffing).

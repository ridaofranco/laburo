At a corporate event or a conference, check-in takes care of the entrance. What happens next (getting to the right room, finding the speaker, learning that the 3 p.m. talk has moved to another hall) depends on reception. This guide explains what an event hostess, host or receptionist does, what profile is asked for and how to prepare. At the end there's a section for people who hire.

## What an event hostess or receptionist does

The name changes with the event: hostess, host, receptionist, greeter. The core job is the same: making sure every guest knows where they are, where they need to go and who to ask.

- **Welcoming.** Greeting guests and getting them oriented as soon as they pass check-in or enter the hall.
- **Guiding to rooms and areas.** Pointing out or accompanying guests to the session room, their assigned table, the cloakroom, catering, the sponsors' area.
- **Looking after speakers and special guests.** Receiving them at a separate point, taking them to the speakers' room or backstage, telling coordination they've arrived and being on hand if they need anything.
- **Giving information about the agenda.** Knowing which talk is in each room and at what time, when the break is and when the event ends.
- **Handling last-minute changes.** A room that moves, a speaker running late, a session that's cancelled. Reception is the first to hear the guest's question and has to have the answer or know who to ask.
- **Keeping order at moments of movement.** Room changes, going out for a break, entering a plenary: that's when the hostess manages the flow and stops people wandering around.

## How it differs from promo staff and check-in staff

These are three jobs that often get mixed up, and it's worth separating them in a brief:

- **Promo staff:** work for a brand. They communicate a product, do sampling, collect data or liven up a stand. The brand sets their goal.
- **Check-in staff:** are at the door. They confirm the person is registered and hand over the badge for their category. The work is fast, precise and concentrated at the arrival rush. It's explained in [conference registration staff: the role](/en/blog/conference-registration-staff-role).
- **Hostess or reception:** looks after guests once inside and throughout the day. They don't sell anything or control access: they inform, guide and solve.

At small events one person may do check-in and then move to reception. That's fine, but it has to be agreed beforehand, with times for each task.

## The profile asked for

- **Manner.** Friendly without overdoing it, with the same patience for the tenth question as for the first.
- **Judgement.** Knowing what to solve on the spot and what to pass on. If you don't know, don't make it up: "let me check and get back to you" is better than sending someone to the wrong room.
- **Tidiness.** Clean, ironed clothes in whatever code the event asks for, a visible badge, footwear you can stand in for many hours. What's assessed is a neat presentation and manner, not physical appearance.
- **Punctuality.** Reception is called in before opening to walk the venue and receive the day's information.
- **Languages.** At conferences with international guests English is requested, and sometimes Portuguese. What matters is being able to give a clear direction and understand a question, not speaking perfectly. If you speak another language, put it on your profile and your CV.

### Clothing

The client almost always sets it: a provided uniform, formal clothes in one colour, or an instruction like "all black". If it isn't clear, ask before the event. Avoid anything distracting (large accessories, strong perfume) and bring a layer if the hall has strong air conditioning. The usual first-day advice is in [first day working at an event](/en/blog/first-day-working-at-an-event), and more on clothing in [event staff dress code](/en/blog/event-staff-dress-code).

## How to prepare for a conference

1. **Study the agenda.** Rooms, times, breaks, plenaries. If they send it beforehand, read all of it; if not, ask for it.
2. **Study the floor plan.** Where each room is, the toilets, the exits, the cloakroom, catering, the speakers' room and the coordination point.
3. **Walk the venue before opening.** The plan is one thing and the real corridor is another. Walk the routes you'll be pointing out.
4. **Ask about special guests.** Who they are, where they're received, who to tell when they arrive.
5. **Know who to ask.** The name and phone number of the coordinator, and how changes will reach you (group chat, radio, in person).
6. **Carry the agenda with you,** printed or on your phone, so you can answer without hesitating.

## Common mistakes

- **Giving directions without knowing.** Sending someone "over there" without being sure. Better to check and come back.
- **Hearing about changes late.** If the room changed and reception doesn't know, the mistake multiplies with every guest.
- **Staying on your phone.** From a distance it looks as though nobody is attending.
- **Leaving your post without saying.** If you move to accompany someone, make sure coordination knows or someone covers your spot.
- **Treating people differently depending on who asks.** The ordinary attendee and the special guest get the same treatment; what changes is protocol, not friendliness.

## For people who hire

If you're putting together reception for a corporate event or a conference:

- **Separate roles in the brief:** who does check-in, who welcomes, who looks after speakers. That way everyone knows where to be.
- **Send the agenda and the floor plan beforehand,** not on the day.
- **Call people in early enough to walk the venue** before the first guest arrives.
- **Set up a channel for changes** and one person who decides.
- **Ask for the language you need** if you have international guests.

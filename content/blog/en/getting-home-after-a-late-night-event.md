Many events finish when almost nothing is open any more: an end-of-year party, a concert, a wedding, a teardown that drags on. For the people working, the shift doesn't end when the music stops: it ends when you get home. This guide is so you plan the trip home before you accept, not at four in the morning on the pavement.

## Before accepting: two questions

When an offer arrives, look at the hours and the place, and if they aren't clear, ask. These are the two questions that matter most.

1. **What time does my shift end?** Not the event: your shift. If you are on the bar or on cleaning, you may keep working for an hour or more after the last guest leaves. If there is a teardown, ask whether it is part of your job. To understand how shifts are put together, see [shift planning for long events](/en/blog/shift-planning-for-long-events).
2. **How do you get home from there at that time?** A venue on a main road with buses is not the same as a country house on the outskirts. Look the place up on the map before you say yes.

With those two answers, you decide. An event that pays well but leaves you an hour's walk from the first transport may not be worth it, and it is fine to say no. If you are just starting out, [event jobs with no experience](/en/blog/event-jobs-with-no-experience) will also help.

## What to ask whoever hires you

Asking isn't awkward. A serious production company knows what time each role finishes and usually has an answer.

- **The estimated end time of your shift**, in writing in the offer.
- **Whether it includes teardown** or whether you can leave when service ends.
- **Whether they arrange anything for the trip home** (a transfer, a paid ride, a group departure point) or whether it is up to you. Don't assume either way: ask.
- **Who your point of contact is**, to tell if the shift runs over or if you need to leave at an agreed time.

Whatever they tell you, get it in writing. If it changes later, you'll have something to fall back on. More on how the work and pay function in [what event work is like and how pay works](/en/blog/what-event-work-is-like-and-pay).

## Plan the trip home with colleagues

At almost every event there is someone heading your way. Ask at the briefing or in the team group chat.

- **Leave together** to the stop, the station or the main road where you can get a car.
- **Share a ride** if you are going to the same area.
- **Wait for the other person to get on** before you leave.

You don't need to become anyone's friend: it is enough to agree at the start of the night who is going home with whom.

## Tell someone

Before you leave home, give someone you trust:

- the event's address,
- the time you expect to leave,
- how you plan to get home.

And when you get home, let them know. If the shift runs over, send a message with the new time. It takes a minute and saves someone from worrying without knowing where you are.

## Battery, credit and some cash

The basics for getting home have to be charged before you start, because during the shift you won't have time.

- **Battery.** Your phone is your map, your way of ordering a car and your way of letting people know. Bring a power bank if your phone won't last the day.
- **Credit on your transport card.** In Buenos Aires, the SUBE card has an emergency negative balance, but it is best not to count on it to get home. The balance can be checked in the SUBE app, on the Mi SUBE website or by tapping the card at self-service terminals.
- **Transport times.** If you live in Buenos Aires, the City publishes the first and last underground train on each line on its official website. Check it before accepting, not on your way out. Elsewhere, check your local transport timetable.
- **Some cash** in case you need to pay for a ride you hadn't planned.
- **Clothes to change into** if the night is cold or the uniform isn't for the street. What each role asks for is in [event staff dress code](/en/blog/event-staff-dress-code).

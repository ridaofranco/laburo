A wedding is a long event, with clearly marked moments (arrival, dinner, toast, dancing, departure) and several suppliers who already bring their own people. That's why the most common mistake isn't being short of staff, but having too many in one place and too few in another. This guide covers the roles, how to work out each one and what to ask beforehand so you don't pay twice for the same thing.

## First: what the venue provides and what the caterer provides

Before working anything out, ask each supplier in writing what staff they include. Often the venue provides cleaning and security, and the caterer provides waiters, kitchen staff and sometimes the bar. What you need to know from each:

- **Which roles and how many people** the price includes.
- **During what hours**: from when until when, including teardown.
- **Who coordinates them** on the day and who you talk to.
- **Uniform**, so any staff you add don't look out of place.
- **What's not included**: cloakroom, reception, cocktail bar, cleaning the toilets during the party.

With that list you build a simple table: role, who covers it, hours. Whatever is left empty is what you hire. If you're getting quotes from several suppliers, [how to request quotes from event suppliers](/en/blog/how-to-request-quotes-from-event-suppliers) explains what to put in writing.

## The roles, one by one

### Reception

Welcomes guests, directs them (where the cloakroom, the ceremony and the toilets are) and, if there's a guest list or seating plan, helps them find their table. Its peak is arrival: people don't all come in at once, but they bunch up in the busiest half hour. Work out how many guests arrive in that stretch and how long one person takes to look after each group; that tells you how many posts need to be staffed at once. After arrival, reception can move to another role if you agreed that. More on the profile in [corporate event hostess and reception](/en/blog/corporate-event-hostess-and-reception).

### Waiters

This is the role that depends most on the type of service. Plated service at the table, food stations, cocktail-style with trays or a mix each need different teams. The serious way to work it out:

1. **Divide the room into sections** of tables one person can serve.
2. **Measure how long a waiter takes** to serve their section at a similar event or at the tasting with the caterer.
3. **Decide how long each course can take** to reach every table in good condition.
4. From that you get how many sections need to go out in parallel and, therefore, how many waiters.

Don't use a guests-per-waiter number taken from the internet: it changes with the layout, the distance to the kitchen and the menu. The full logic is in [how many staff do you need for an event](/en/blog/how-many-staff-for-an-event).

### Bar

If the caterer doesn't provide the bar, it has to be sized for the peak, which at a wedding is usually the drinks reception and the start of the dancing. What sets the pace is how many drinks per minute each bar position can turn out with your menu: beer and wine are not the same as signature cocktails. Add a barback for ice, glasses and restocking, so the bartender doesn't leave their post. There's more detail in [hiring bartenders for a corporate event](/en/blog/hiring-bartenders-for-a-corporate-event).

### Cloakroom

In winter it's essential, and in summer a small point for bags is sometimes enough. It's sized for departure, not arrival: at the end of the party everyone wants their coat at the same time. The step by step is in [how to run an event cloakroom](/en/blog/how-to-run-an-event-cloakroom).

### Coordination

One person who doesn't serve, doesn't work the bar and doesn't greet guests: they hold the schedule, talk to the venue, the caterer, the DJ and the organiser, and sort out whatever comes up. They're the one who signals when dinner goes out, when things pause for the toast and when the cloakroom opens for collection. At large weddings it's worth giving them a runner to fetch and carry.

### Cleaning

If the venue doesn't cover it during the party, you need to add at least the toilets and clearing crockery from the side tables. Before, during and after are three different jobs, explained in [cleaning staff for an event](/en/blog/cleaning-staff-for-an-event).

## Long hours and reliefs

Between setup and close, a wedding can keep staff on their feet for many hours. Three rules:

- **Breaks go in the quiet moments**, usually during the dancing, and one at a time per section. Never during arrival, dinner or the toast.
- **If the day is too long for one person, set up a second shift** for the close instead of stretching the first. Someone arriving fresh at one in the morning performs better than someone who has been on for ten hours.
- **A meal for staff** at a fixed time, before the main service. Agree it with the caterer.

How to build the schedule, with overlap between shifts for the handover, is in [shift planning for long events](/en/blog/shift-planning-for-long-events).

## What to put in writing in each offer

- Arrival time and estimated finishing time, including teardown.
- Specific tasks and who they report to.
- Uniform or dress code.
- Whether there's a staff meal and at what time.
- Pay and conditions.

Whatever isn't written down gets argued about on the day. And have a plan if someone doesn't arrive: [event staff no-show: what to do](/en/blog/event-staff-no-show-what-to-do) sets out a procedure.

## Common mistakes

- **Hiring waiters without asking whether the caterer already provides them.**
- **Sizing the bar for the average** instead of the drinks reception peak.
- **Having no coordinator** who isn't busy doing something else.
- **Closing the cloakroom with the rest of the staff**, when there are still guests left.
- **One shift for everything**, with no breaks and no relief for the close.

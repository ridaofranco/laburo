"How many staff do I need for an event of 500 people?" is the question we always get, and the honest answer is that the number of guests is the least useful figure of all.

We are not going to make up a ratio. We'll give you the calculation that does work, with the four variables behind it. The numbers in the examples are yours to fill in: what matters here is the arithmetic, not the figure.

## The four variables

Every staffing plan comes from multiplying and adding these:

1. **Service points.** How many physical spots must be covered at the same time.
2. **Throughput.** How much each point serves per unit of time.
3. **Shifts.** How many times you need to rotate people to cover the whole duration.
4. **Buffer.** How many extra people you add so a no-show doesn't leave a point empty.

If one of the four is missing, the number you get will be wrong. The one people forget most is the third.

## Step 1: split the event into moments

The first mistake is thinking of staff as one block. It isn't: it's four different operations that overlap.

- **Setup.** Load-in, build, technical crew. Usually the day before or early morning.
- **Check-in and doors.** The shortest and most visible peak.
- **During.** Bars, catering, guest service, cleaning, technical booth.
- **Close and teardown.** Almost always late at night, almost always understaffed.

Each moment has its own headcount, its own call time and often its own people. Calculate each one separately and then add them up. A well-planned team doesn't have everyone on site from the morning.

## Step 2: count points, not guests

Walk the event and write down every spot that needs someone standing there: every door, every bar position, every till, every access to a section, every restroom, every cloakroom point.

That list is your floor. Then add how many people each point needs to run without a queue.

## Step 3: the entrance math

Check-in is the one moment of the event you can calculate precisely, and it's worth doing, because it's where a bad estimate is visible from the street.

You need three numbers:

- **How many people** have to get in.
- **In how many minutes** you want them in.
- **How fast one check-in point works**, measured in people per minute.

The math: people ÷ minutes = people per minute you must process. Then: people per minute ÷ rate of one point = points working at once.

Example, just to see the mechanics: if 800 people have to get in within 40 minutes, you need to process 20 per minute. If one point validates 4 per minute, you need 5 points working in parallel.

**The rate per point is yours, not ours.** Scanning a QR code is not the same as looking up a surname on a printed list, and neither is the same as also handing out a wristband. Measure it once at your own event and that number will serve you for good.

The same logic applies to the bar: estimate how many drinks are served per hour at the peak and divide it by what one bar position serves per hour. You supply both numbers.

## Step 4: multiply by shifts

This is where most calculations fall apart. **The number of people working at once is not the number of people you need to hire.**

If you need 5 check-in points and the event has a single 40-minute opening, you hire for that slot. But if you need 6 people behind the bar and the event lasts ten hours, that's not 6 people: it's 6 positions covered for ten hours, and nobody performs the same in hour nine as in hour two.

Add the **relief staff** for meals and breaks. If you don't count them, they come out of the same team and you get empty positions right at the peak.

## Step 5: the buffer

Someone won't show up. That's a fact of the trade, not an accident.

You set the buffer, but with a clear criterion: **critical roles never run with the exact headcount.** Entrance, till and bar are the three places where a missing person is noticed in thirty seconds. In cleaning or general service you have more room.

## Write the headcount down before you look for people

The calculation is useless if it lives in your head. Before you send the first message you should have a table of **role and quantity**: 10 waiters, 4 bartenders, 5 at the entrance, 6 for setup.

That distinction is what saves the most money. An offer that was **sent** is not a confirmed person. An offer that was **seen** isn't either. Each offer has its real status (sent, seen, accepted, declined or expired) and only accepted ones count as a covered spot. Declined and expired offers go back to being open spots, which is the only way to find out in time that you're short.

## The checklist

- Did I separate setup, check-in, during and teardown?
- Did I count the service points for each moment?
- Did I do the entrance math with my own measured rate?
- Did I multiply by shifts and add relief staff?
- Did I leave a buffer in critical roles?
- Do I have the headcount written down by role and quantity?
- Do I know how many of those spots are truly confirmed?

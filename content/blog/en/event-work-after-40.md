There is a widespread idea that events are for twenty-somethings who can handle late nights. Part of the work is like that. But there are many roles where whoever hires prefers someone who has already worked with the public, has managed people or knows how to handle a problem without calling anyone. This guide is for you if you are over 40 and want to get into, or back into, event work.

## The roles where experience counts

Not every role asks for the same thing. These are the ones that most value having worked before, at events or elsewhere:

- **Staff coordination.** Organising a group, assigning tasks, covering for whoever is missing and talking to production. If you have managed a team in a shop, a factory or an office, you have already done a good part of this. More detail in [when you need an event staff coordinator](/en/blog/when-you-need-an-event-staff-coordinator).
- **Registration.** Checking lists, handing out badges, dealing with someone who isn't on the list without creating a queue. It takes order and calm with people in a hurry. See [what conference registration staff do](/en/blog/conference-registration-staff-role).
- **Reception and hostess work at corporate events.** Presence, formal manners, knowing who to pass each thing to. See [corporate event hostess and reception](/en/blog/corporate-event-hostess-and-reception).
- **Cloakroom.** A system, tidiness and patience at the departure peak. See [how to run an event cloakroom](/en/blog/how-to-run-an-event-cloakroom).
- **Logistics.** Receiving suppliers, checking what comes in and goes out, organising storerooms. If you have worked in a warehouse or in purchasing, that carries over.
- **Technical.** Sound, lighting, electrics, screens, structures. Here experience is almost everything. See [event sound and lighting technician jobs](/en/blog/event-sound-and-lighting-technician-jobs).

What they have in common: they are roles where a mistake is costly and whoever hires is looking for someone who won't get overwhelmed.

## How to describe your track record on your profile

The most common mistake is putting only the title of your previous job ("administrative assistant", "shop manager") and expecting the reader to translate it. They won't. Do it yourself.

On your LABURO profile there are fields for your roles, whether you have event experience, your years of experience, a text to describe your experience and another about you. Use them like this:

1. **Choose the roles where your experience helps**, even if you have never done them at an event. If you dealt with the public for ten years, reception and registration make sense.
2. **In the experience text, write tasks, not job titles.** For example: "I served customers at a counter, managed queues at peak times, supervised 6 people and drew up the weekly rota." That reads in ten seconds and says exactly what you can do.
3. **If you have already worked at events, name the type of event**, not the client: conference, wedding, trade show, concert.
4. **In the "about you" field, one or two lines on how you work.** Something verifiable, not adjectives: "I arrive early, I give notice in good time if I can't make it, I don't leave until it's closed."

For the rest of the profile (photo, details, CV), see the guide on [what to include in your event staff profile](/en/blog/event-staff-profile-what-to-include).

## Real availability, not the one that sounds good

After 40 it is common to have another job, children, or someone to care for. That is no problem. The problem is marking yourself as always available and then turning down half the offers, or worse, confirming and pulling out.

On your profile you can indicate whether you are available at weekends, whether you can travel and how much notice you need to take a job. Mark what you can really deliver. If you can only do it with a week's notice, say so: you'll get fewer offers, but the ones that arrive you'll be able to take.

Also bear in mind an event's real hours. Many include early setup or teardown in the small hours, and that isn't always in the role title. Before accepting, read the offer's conditions and ask what time it ends. If you have to leave late, see [getting home after a late-night event](/en/blog/getting-home-after-a-late-night-event). And if your body is a factor, choose roles that suit it: reception is not the same as teardown. That is what [event setup and teardown: how to work safely](/en/blog/event-setup-and-teardown-work-safely) is for.

## How to compete without cutting your rate

The temptation is to charge less so you get chosen. It works once and leaves you in the worst position: competing with anyone over who charges least. What gets you called again is something else:

- **Arriving before the call time.** The coordinator notices at the first event.
- **Not pulling out after confirming.** If you really can't make it, give notice in good time and, if you can, suggest someone. On the other side, a last-minute dropout is the worst problem of the day (as [what to do when event staff don't show up](/en/blog/event-staff-no-show-what-to-do) explains).
- **Solving things without escalating everything.** It is exactly what's expected of someone with experience.
- **Being clear about your rate from the start.** If the offer doesn't work for you, reply respectfully and say what you think is fair. To understand how pay works at events, see [what event work is like and how pay works](/en/blog/what-event-work-is-like-and-pay).

## If you have never worked at events

Don't start with the most demanding role. A first event in registration, reception or cloakroom shows you how an event works from the inside: schedules, briefings, how people talk on the radio, who decides what. With that you can aim for coordination or logistics. For the first day, there is [first day working at an event](/en/blog/first-day-working-at-an-event), and if you are worried about having no background in the industry, [event jobs with no experience](/en/blog/event-jobs-with-no-experience).

A concert or live show packs a lot into a few hours: the audience arrives almost all at once, the band has a time slot that doesn't move and the technical crew needs space to load in and out without anyone getting in the way. This guide covers the positions you almost always need, how to calculate them by measured pace and how to organise communication so the team doesn't get lost in the venue. If your event is in the daytime and outdoors, [outdoor festival staffing](/en/blog/outdoor-festival-staffing) will help too.

## The positions at a show

The list comes from the venue's floor plan and the show's schedule. Every door, every bar and every area is a position someone has to cover.

- **Door and ticket control.** Scanning tickets, wristbands, separate queues for guests and press. It's the audience's first contact and the tensest moment of the night. If there's admission and stay control, whoever does it has to comply with the rules: it's explained in [the guide to Argentina's Law 26,370 for door staff](/en/blog/argentina-law-26370-door-staff). And if you need private security, see [private security at events in Buenos Aires](/en/blog/private-security-at-events-buenos-aires).
- **Floor and aisles.** They direct the audience, keep aisles and exits clear, and tell production if something gets complicated in an area. In venues with numbered seating, they show each person to their seat.
- **Bar.** Bartenders and barbacks, with restocking of ice, cups and drinks. The breaks between bands are their peak. How to work out how many you need is in [hiring bartenders for a corporate event](/en/blog/hiring-bartenders-for-a-corporate-event).
- **Dressing rooms and production.** Someone to receive the artists, control who goes into the dressing rooms, have the catering ready and know who to call for each thing. If the show has several bands, the person who runs the stage is the [stage manager](/en/blog/what-is-a-stage-manager-at-an-event).
- **Runners.** They fetch and carry whatever is missing: a cable, a box of cups, a charged radio, change. They stop people from leaving their post to go and get something. More detail in [what does an event runner do](/en/blog/what-does-an-event-runner-do).
- **Load-in and load-out with the tech crew.** It starts hours before opening and ends when the last piece of equipment leaves. They work in coordination with sound, lighting and backline, who decide what moves first and what isn't to be touched. How to look after yourself is in [event setup and teardown: how to work safely](/en/blog/event-setup-and-teardown-work-safely).

## The two peaks: entry and exit

A show has two moments that shape the whole set-up.

1. **Entry.** The audience arrives concentrated in the last hour before the show. If the door can't cope, the queue is still outside when the band has already started, and that's the first complaint of the night. Opening doors in good time and separating queues by ticket type spreads the peak.
2. **Exit.** At the end almost everyone leaves together. Aisles, the cloakroom if there is one and the doors need to be covered until the last person is out. If there's a cloakroom, it's sized for the exit: explained in [how to run an event cloakroom](/en/blog/how-to-run-an-event-cloakroom).

The most common mistake is planning the team for the night's average. The average doesn't exist: there are peaks and there's dead time.

## How to calculate by measured pace

There's no number of people per post that works for every venue. It depends on the type of ticket, whether there are bag searches, how many doors the venue has and the team's experience. What you can do is measure and do the maths.

1. **Estimate how many people come in during the last hour before the show.** If you have data from a similar show at the same venue, use it.
2. **Decide how many minutes you want entry to take.**
3. **Time the real pace of one control point**, from when the person shows their ticket until they're through. Take the average in a trial or at a previous show.
4. **Divide:** people ÷ minutes = people per minute you need. That number ÷ the pace of one point = points that have to work at once.

Do the same for the bar during breaks and for the exit. The full logic, with shifts and buffer, is in [how many staff do you need for an event](/en/blog/how-many-staff-for-an-event). If the show is long or has many bands, add reliefs using the schedule in [shift planning for long events](/en/blog/shift-planning-for-long-events).

## Communication: radio and meeting point

At a show there's noise, little light and people everywhere. Communication is sorted out before the doors open.

- **Radio by area.** Who has a radio, which channel each area uses and who listens to everything. Not everyone needs one: one lead per area talking to production is enough.
- **Short, agreed words.** To ask for backup, report that an area is full or that someone needs attention. They're reviewed at the briefing.
- **Charged radios and spares.** A runner can take care of swapping batteries during the night.
- **A fixed meeting point.** A place the whole team knows, away from where the audience moves around. It's used for the briefing, for reliefs and to gather people if something fails.
- **A plan if someone doesn't turn up.** Which position is left uncovered first and who covers it. For that, see [event staff no-show: what to do](/en/blog/event-staff-no-show-what-to-do).

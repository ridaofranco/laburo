If you have already worked as a waiter in a restaurant, at an event you'll use almost everything you know. What changes is the pace: instead of tables ordering each in their own time, you have a whole room eating together. This guide covers the service formats you'll come across, what the people hiring expect and how to set up your profile so dates come your way.

## What changes compared with a restaurant

In a restaurant the work is by table and by order. At an event it is **by course and all at once**:

- **A fixed menu.** You don't take orders; you know in advance what goes out and in what order.
- **Everyone at once.** The starter is served across the whole room at the same time, then the main, then dessert. If one table falls behind, it shows.
- **Coordination with the kitchen.** The floor coordinator calls when to go out; nobody goes out on their own.
- **A shift longer than the service.** You arrive early to set up (table linen, cutlery, glasses, napkins) and stay afterwards to clear and close.
- **A team you may not know.** You often work with new people at each event, so communication has to be short and clear.

## Service formats

### Course-by-course service

The classic at weddings, company dinners and galas. Each waiter is assigned one or more tables. One course is served, cleared, then the next is served. What matters is **following the coordinator's signal** and serving and clearing evenly across your section.

### Plated service

Plates come out of the kitchen ready and you take them to the table. It looks like the simplest format, but it takes care: carrying several plates without touching the presentation, setting each one down from the right side and the right way round, and not dawdling so the food doesn't go cold.

### Banquet service

Used at large events. It can be synchronised table service or platters from which you serve each guest. What matters here is **synchronisation**: lots of waiters enter the room together and leave together, like choreography.

### Cocktail service with trays

Guests stand and you circulate with a tray offering canapés or drinks. There is no table: you have an area. What's valued:

- **Knowing what you're offering.** If someone asks what a canapé is, you need to be able to say, especially if they ask about allergens or whether it is gluten-free. If you don't know, ask in the kitchen before you go out.
- **Posture and a steady tray.** One hand for the tray, the other free for napkins.
- **Restocking without leaving your area empty.** You coordinate with your colleagues so someone is always circulating.
- **Clearing.** Used glasses and napkins are part of your job too.

## What's expected

What comes up most when waiters are hired for events:

- **Punctuality.** Setup starts at a set time and the room has to be ready before the first guest arrives. Being late at an event is more serious than in a restaurant: there is no slack.
- **Uniform.** Set by whoever hires you. It is often a white or black shirt, black trousers and closed black shoes, but always confirm it beforehand. Ironed and clean.
- **A well-kept appearance.** Long hair tied back, short clean nails, no strong perfume.
- **Knowing the steps of service** and the basic terms (which side you serve from, which side you clear from).
- **Teamwork** with the kitchen, the bar and the runners.

## The food handler certificate

In Argentina, article 21 of the Argentine Food Code requires a food handler certificate for anyone who is or could be in contact with food. According to ANMAT, the Argentine certificate lasts three years and is valid nationwide. A waiter serving food is in that situation, so it is worth keeping it up to date and adding it to your profile. If you work outside Argentina, the name and requirements depend on your country or city.

How to get it, how long it lasts and what the regulation says is in [food handler certificate for event work](/en/blog/food-handler-certificate-for-event-work).

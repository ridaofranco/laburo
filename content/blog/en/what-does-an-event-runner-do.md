At almost every event there's someone with no fixed post who is nevertheless everywhere: fetching the box left in the storeroom, taking water up to the dressing room, running a cable to the stage, walking a speaker to the room. That person is the runner. It isn't the most visible job, but it's one of the ones that teaches you the most, which is why it's one of the best ways into the industry.

## What a runner does

The runner sorts out the small things so nobody else has to leave their spot. The tasks change from event to event, but these almost always come up:

- **Production errands:** buying or picking up whatever is missing (tape, batteries, printouts, an adapter), taking papers from one area to another, passing on a message in person when the radio isn't enough.
- **Moving materials:** carrying boxes, merchandise, badges, signage or set pieces between the storeroom, the main room and the loading area.
- **Dressing room catering:** restocking water, coffee and food in the dressing rooms or the speakers' room, and making sure each artist has what was on their list.
- **Moving people:** escorting artists, speakers or guests from the entrance to the dressing room and from the dressing room to the stage, on time.
- **Supporting the technical crew and the stage manager:** handing over a microphone, finding a technician, taking the updated running order, letting them know someone is ready to go on.

At some events the runner works right next to production; at others they're assigned to the stage or the bar. That's why it pays to ask on day one who you report to and which area you'll be in.

## What is expected of a runner

The job doesn't require technical knowledge, but it does require a way of working:

- **Move fast without getting lost.** An errand that takes twice as long holds up whoever asked for it.
- **Phone charged all day.** People will text and call you. Bring a power bank and a cable.
- **Know the venue.** As soon as you arrive, walk around: entrances, storeroom, dressing rooms, stage, toilets, emergency exit, loading area. A runner who knows where everything is is worth double.
- **Understand the first time and confirm.** Repeat the request in one sentence ("two waters to dressing room 3, on it") and say when it's done.
- **Discretion backstage.** You'll see artists, speakers and clients up close. No photos, no asking for anything, no talking about what happens inside, either at the time or later on social media.
- **Be able to say "I don't know, I'll ask".** A question is better than a wrong errand.

## A typical day, before, during and after

### Before

Runners are usually called early, for the setup. You arrive, introduce yourself to your supervisor, get your badge and, if needed, a radio. The first few hours are about moving things: bringing materials down, organising the storeroom, setting up dressing rooms with what each person asked for, putting the signage in place. It's the best moment to walk the venue and note where everything is. In [what happens on your first day working at an event](/en/blog/first-day-working-at-an-event) you'll find the call times, what to bring and the three questions worth asking when you arrive.

### During

When the event opens, the pace changes: requests come in several at a time and they're all urgent. You restock dressing rooms, walk whoever is going on stage, bring what the technical crew needs, look for someone who has gone missing. Having the schedule at hand helps you get ahead: if you know a speaker goes on in twenty minutes, you can already be at their dressing room.

### After

During teardown, the runner becomes part of logistics again: clearing dressing rooms, gathering materials, returning equipment and badges, loading what goes back to the storeroom. Before you leave, tell your supervisor you're done and pass on anything relevant (something that ran out, something that broke, a request left pending).

## Why it's the best way in

The runner sees the whole event. In a single day they pass through production, the stage, the technical crew, catering and access control, and meet the people in each area. Almost no other entry-level role gives you that.

On top of that, a runner who responds well gets noticed quickly: they're the one production tells "come back next time". If you've never worked at an event, [how to get your first event job with no experience](/en/blog/event-jobs-with-no-experience) explains how to put together your first profile.

## Where it leads

- **Production assistant.** The most natural step: you already know the pace, the suppliers and how a schedule is built. What's missing is making small decisions on your own and organising tasks for others.
- **Field producer or logistics coordinator.** If your work was mostly moving materials and organising the storeroom, that's the path.
- **Stage manager.** Whoever works as the stage runner learns how cues are given, how to get a speaker on stage on time and how to talk to the technical crew. It's one of the paths we explain in [what is a stage manager at an event](/en/blog/what-is-a-stage-manager-at-an-event).
- **Technical crew.** If you spent the day next to sound or lighting, it may spark that interest: stagehand or setup work are good first steps.

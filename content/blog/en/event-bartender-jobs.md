If you have already worked behind a bar, you have the trade. What changes at an event is everything else: the place, the menu, the pace and who hires you. This guide is for anyone who wants to work as a bartender at events: what's different, what you'll be asked for, which paperwork is worth having and how to set up your profile so people find you.

## Event bar and regular bar: what changes

In a bar you know the counter by heart: where every bottle is, how much ice goes on a Friday, what the regular orders. At an event, none of that is a given.

- **The bar is set up that day.** It might be a trestle table at an outdoor site, a mobile bar in a function room or a counter on a booth. You arrive, organise it and make it work.
- **The menu is short and you didn't choose it.** The brand, the production company or the company that outsourced the bar decides it. Your job is to execute it quickly and always the same way.
- **Guests arrive in rushes.** At opening, at the interval, after the show. Between rushes, you restock and clean.
- **The team changes.** You often work with people you don't know and with a supervisor you are introduced to that day.
- **The hours are the event's.** That includes arriving early to set up and staying afterwards to break down, if that was agreed.

## What's expected

**Speed without getting sloppy.** In a rush, the queue measures you by how long you take. But a badly made drink or a wet, messy bar shows just the same.

**Mise en place ready before opening.** Ice, glasses, cut fruit, garnishes, bottles in place, barback to hand. If the bar opens and you are still slicing lemons, you are already behind. Ask beforehand what time you need to arrive to set up.

**The menu by heart.** Read it before the event if it is sent to you, and ask about measures: at many events the brand wants every drink to come out identical.

**Cashing up, if it applies.** Not every bar takes payment: at many corporate events the bar is open. If it does take payment, you may be asked to count the cash, check the tickets or reconcile card payments at the end. If so, it should be in the offer, and it is best to do the count with your supervisor present.

**Manners.** An event guest is not your regular customer: they are the guest of a brand or a company. Patience with the queue, a friendly answer when something isn't available, and no arguing. If someone gets difficult, tell your supervisor or security.

**Teamwork.** If you work with barbacks, they restock and you make the drinks. Coordinating that is what makes a bar perform.

## The food handler certificate

Behind a bar you handle ice, fruit and garnishes, so it is common to be asked for a food handler certificate. Having it up to date qualifies you for more hospitality offers and is worth having on your profile. What it is, who requires it and how to get it in Argentina is in [food handler certificate for event work](/en/blog/food-handler-certificate-for-event-work); in other countries, the name and requirements depend on your country or city.

## Alcohol and minors: the rule is not negotiable

In Argentina, Law 24,788 against Alcoholism bans the sale of any kind of alcoholic drink to anyone under eighteen throughout the national territory (article 1). It doesn't depend on what the event says or on the bar being open. If you have doubts about someone's age, you don't serve them and you pass it to your supervisor: it is the decision that protects you and the event.

The same law bans contests or events that require drinking alcohol in a way that distorts tasting (article 7). If you are asked to set up something like that behind the bar, speak up.

## How you look from the other side

Whoever hires bartenders for an event is asking how many bars to set up, what profile to ask for and what each side has to provide. Reading that point of view helps you prepare your answers: it is in [hiring bartenders for a corporate event](/en/blog/hiring-bartenders-for-a-corporate-event). Before you confirm, make sure you are clear on:

- **The full hours**, including setup and breakdown.
- **What you need to bring**: your own tools, clothing, apron.
- **Whether there is a till** and who cashes up.
- **The pay** and how it is paid.

If any of that isn't in the offer, ask before accepting.
